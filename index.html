GUIDE • HOW IT WORKS

A quick manual for the sim and its numbers. Design → BUILD & DYNO → drive it.
The Loop
1. DESIGN an engine (bottom end, air path, fuel & spark, cooling…), then press BUILD & DYNO. 2. DYNO reads the full power/torque curve and a graded scorecard. 3. ENGINE is a live test bench: you set THROTTLE and LOAD and watch the engine respond in real time. You never set RPM directly — you drive it.
Driving Controls
THROTTLE — opens the intake; raises manifold pressure → torque → revs.
LOAD (dyno brake) — resistance that sets the hold RPM. 0% = free-rev to the limiter; 100% = holds near idle. It only resists above its target, so any engine can always rev up to it.
ELEC LOAD — accessory draw (lights/AC). Heavy load + small alternator at idle drains the battery → low voltage → weak spark.
AFR — air-fuel ratio. Peak power near λ0.86 (rich), best economy near λ1.05 (lean).
IAT / COOLANT / OIL — charge & engine temps (auto in AUTO THERMAL mode).
NITROUS — if fitted, sprays at wide-open throttle from a bottle that depletes.
Reading the Numbers
HP / kW — power = torque × rpm. TORQUE (Nm) — twisting force; peaks lower than power.
BMEP (bar) — brake mean effective pressure: torque normalised to displacement, so you can compare engines of any size. VE (%) — volumetric efficiency: how well the engine breathes vs its swept volume (boost can exceed 100%).
THERMAL EFF (%) — share of fuel energy turned into work. BSFC (g/kWh) — fuel burned per unit power; lower is better (best around stoich, part-load).
FUEL FLOW (L/h) — live consumption. MAP / BOOST (bar) — manifold pressure; boost is MAP above 1.0.
KNOCK (LOW/MED/HIGH) — detonation risk. AUTO timing retards to control it; sustained HIGH does damage. HEALTH (%) — condition; falls under abuse, and 0 = a blown engine (rebuild).
EMISSIONS (CLEAN/OK/DIRTY) — from the exhaust analyser: CO & HC climb rich, NOx climbs lean/hot; a catalytic converter only cleans all three near stoich.
λ (lambda) — AFR ÷ the fuel's stoichiometric AFR: λ<1 rich, 1 = stoich, >1 lean.
COST ($) — total build price of the parts you chose. REL (reliability) — a design estimate of how much abuse the build invites (boost/knock/heat/high-rpm stress); higher is tougher. hp/$1k — power per thousand dollars: the value metric.
The Scorecard
HOT LAP REPORT — on the TRACK tab, LAP REPORT PNG / PDF saves the lap as a telemetry sheet: the circuit map with full-throttle and braking sections picked out and every corner tagged, a speed trace against distance with the braking zones shaded, lap and sector times, the full car spec, and a speed trap at every corner — entry, apex and exit speed, gear and radius. Corners are numbered T1…Tn and named per circuit (Riverside's are Millrace, Weir, Ferryman…). "Full throttle" means the car is accelerating at its limit, engine or tyres; the footer also reports how much of the lap is specifically engine-limited.
DYNO REPORT — the REPORT PNG / REPORT PDF buttons save the current pull as a printable sheet laid out like a real dynamometer print-out: the power and torque traces with their peaks called out, a peak-result band, the full engine specification, the conditions the run was made under, and a tabulated run-data list. Any pulls you've logged are overlaid faintly for comparison. The graph is the engine as tuned right now, so the sheet also prints the design potential (the best-power figure the scorecard grades) — that's why the two numbers can differ. Everything is generated offline in the browser; nothing is uploaded.
Every build is graded S → F from seven things: specific power & torque (per litre), powerband width, thermal efficiency, knock safety, driveability (low-end torque) and emit (emissions). Chasing peak power usually costs drivability, safety and emissions — the score rewards a balanced build.
Career & Budget
Track objectives judge the whole car, not just the engine: four of them set a lap target on a named circuit (Club Sprint at Ashdown, Grand Prix Pace at Riverside, Under Seven at the Ring on the Nordschleife, and Lap per Dollar for the cheapest car that can still lap quickly). Their budget covers engine plus vehicle, so a cheap engine in an expensive chassis counts against you — build the car on the VEHICLE tab, then watch the objective card.
The scorecard also reports your lap time for the circuit selected on the TRACK tab. It is reported, not folded into the letter grade: the grade rates the engine, and an engine's grade should not move because you fitted different tyres.
Pick an OBJECTIVE at the top of the DESIGN tab to turn free building into a puzzle. Each challenge sets targets — power, economy, emissions, reliability or power-per-dollar — and some cap your budget. The card ticks off each target live (✓/✗) and shows a PASSED verdict when the whole brief is met; the build's cost and reliability are always on show. Cheap displacement wins on value; boost and race parts win on power but cost money and reliability. Balance the brief.
Engine Types
Petrol 4-stroke — the all-rounder: spark ignition, one power stroke every two revs.
Diesel (CI) — compression ignition (no spark, no spark-knock), very high compression, runs lean and unthrottled. Big low-end torque, low redline, and the best efficiency — but heavy, and its emissions signature is NOx + soot (PM), cleaned by a DPF/SCR system.
2-stroke — a power stroke every rev (ported, no valvetrain): high specific output for its size but thirsty and HC-dirty (it burns oil and short-circuits fresh charge).
Rotary (Wankel) — a spinning rotor firing every rev: turbine-smooth, revs sky-high, high power per litre, but thirsty, HC-heavy and hard on apex seals (lower reliability).
Switching type retargets the compression, redline and AFR ranges and disables what doesn't apply (spark for diesel; cam/VVT for ported engines).
Design Subsystems
Bottom end — engine type, bore × stroke × cylinders = displacement; oversquare & higher redline shift the powerband up.
Air path — turbo (spools with rpm; size/config trade lag vs top-end) or supercharger (boost from idle); intercooler size cools the charge (denser, less knock) with a little lag; exhaust and catalytic converter trade flow vs back-pressure/emissions; nitrous is bottled power.
Boost is not free, and the frame has a size. A compressor makes pressure by spinning, and that shaft speed has to be bought with exhaust energy — so the more boost you ask a given turbo for, the later it arrives and the longer it takes to build. The same small frame that is fully lit by 1200 rpm at 0.5 bar does not reach half of 2.5 bar until nearly 3000 rpm. The spool figure quoted in the design summary is for the boost you actually asked for, not a fixed property of the frame.
Each frame also has a rated flow — small 32, medium 55, large 82 lb/min, and multi-turbo configs flow more (twin ×1.85, sequential ×1.55, compound ×2.3). That is the axis a real compressor map is drawn against. Ask an engine to move more air than its turbo is rated for and you are off the map: compressor efficiency falls away, the top end chokes off, and — the one that really bites — the charge arrives hot. Squeezing air heats it by T·(PR0.283 − 1) / efficiency, which grows faster than the boost does and grows again as efficiency drops. A 2.0 L asking 2.5 bar of a small frame wants 1.7× its rated flow, and the charge reaches the valves at 74 °C where the right frame delivers 58 °C — so it knocks, makes 120 hp less than the medium frame, and is slower round a lap than the same engine at 0.8 bar. Nothing stops you building it; it simply isn't the way to make power.
One limit: spool lag — the time to build boost — is modelled on the live ENGINE bench, but the performance and lap figures are solved from the steady boost available at each rpm, so they feel the spool point moving and not the transient. A very laggy build is therefore flattered slightly by its 0–100 and lap times.
Induction — carbs → EFI → ITBs set breathing, metering precision and idle quality.
Valvetrain — wilder cams move power up top & idle lumpy; VVT restores the bottom end. Valves per cylinder, valve material and springs are now modelled from real geometry rather than assumed. Four valves flow about 22% more than two for the same bore (and five flow no more than four — which is why nobody builds them any more). The app derives the valve diameter your bore can physically fit, works out what that valve weighs, and tells you the valve float rpm — the speed at which the spring can no longer pull the valve back down and it stops following the cam. If that number is below your redline, the engine cannot use the rev range it claims. Fit stiffer springs, lighter titanium valves, less lift, or lower the redline. Big-bore two-valve engines also choke on inlet airflow near the limiter, which is the real reason a big pushrod V8 makes its power low and a four-valve screamer can rev.
Fuel & spark — fuel type, octane, injector, ignition type, FIXED vs AUTO (ECU) timing, and the fuel mixture: MANUAL (you set one AFR) or AUTO, a 3D ECU map that runs near stoich at light load and enriches under load/boost for power — just like a real fuel map. In AUTO you can tune the map: tap any cell to select it, then − LEAN / + RICH to shift that load×rpm target (RESET MAP restores the factory grid).
Cooling & electrical — radiator/fan/oil-cooler/thermostat set temps; alternator size and ignition type set charging and the misfire RPM limit.
The ENGINE tab is a live cutaway of the engine you just designed. Everything on it moves because the simulation moved it. It is deliberately not drawn in real time: a crank at 5000 rpm turns 83 times a second, which at 60 fps is a strobe rather than a picture — you could not watch a valve open. So the cycle runs at a pace you can read, between about 3.3 s and 1.1 s per full four-stroke cycle, still ranked by rpm so idle is visibly lazier than the limiter. The true speed is on the tachometer above it and in the note; the animation's job is to make the cycle legible. The piston follows proper crank-slider geometry (not a sine wave — a real rod of finite length makes the piston linger at the bottom and snap through the top), the bore is drawn to your actual bore/stroke ratio so an oversquare engine really is short and wide, and the valves open on true four-stroke timing with the scavenging overlap visible around TDC. The chamber is colour-coded by what is in it right now — fresh charge, compressed charge, burning, burnt — and the stroke is named above the head, so the cycle is something you can watch rather than something you have to remember. A two-stroke shows ports uncovering in the liner instead of valves; a rotary shows a real epitrochoid housing with the rotor turning at a third of the eccentric shaft. The strip beside it draws every cylinder at its own phase, so you can see the firing order run through a V12.
The numbers that used to be printed here in 7 px type now live in the LIVE OUTPUT panel beside the canvas, where they are actual text; what stays on the cutaway is what benefits from being next to the part it describes — throttle plate angle, boost and compressor flow at the turbo, EGT at the exhaust, and the knock bar.
Vehicle & Performance
On the VEHICLE tab you drop your engine into a car. Pick a chassis and it arrives on its factory spec — the tyres, wheels, brakes, suspension, aero and gearbox that car would really wear, so a kei car turns up on skinny eco tyres and small steel discs while a hypercar gets wide semi-slicks, carbon-ceramics and a wing/diffuser package. Everything stays editable from there; REFIT FACTORY SPEC puts it all back.
The GARAGE at the bottom of the tab keeps as many finished cars as you like. Name one and press SAVE TO GARAGE and the whole thing is stored together — the chassis, every part bolted to it, and the engine inside, down to a hand-tuned fuel map. Each card shows the headline numbers it had when you saved it (power, weight, 0–100, top speed, lap time and total cost), so you can line three builds up and compare them without rebuilding any of them. LOAD puts a car back exactly as it was; typing a name that already exists highlights that card and saving overwrites it. This is separate from SAVE/LOAD, which is a single quicksave slot that each save overwrites. A garage entry is the same record SAVE FILE writes and a share link carries, so a build is portable between all three. Everything lives in this browser only — clearing site data clears the garage, and SAVE FILE is what makes a build survive that.
From that baseline you tune the drivetrain (FWD/RWD/AWD — AWD launches hardest), the gearbox (type, speeds and the gearing slider), tyres (compound + width/profile/ diameter set grip and rolling radius), aero (front/rear/floor add downforce and drag), brakes (type + rotor size set stopping power and fade resistance) and suspension (a mechanical-grip multiplier).
Gearbox type changes four things: how long the drive is cut on an upshift, how much of the engine's output survives the driveline, how the ratios are spaced, and how the car leaves the line.
· Manual (H-pattern) — the most efficient box, but you lose ~350 ms per shift lifting off, moving the lever and re-engaging. Cheap. Best where there are few shifts to make.
· Sequential — a race box that bangs the next dog in: ~60 ms shifts and close ratios that keep a peaky engine in its band. Expensive.
· Dual-clutch (DCT) — the next gear is already engaged on the second clutch, so shifts are near-seamless (~30 ms). The quickest thing here, and priced like it.
· Torque-converter auto — slowest shifts of the modern boxes (~200 ms) and the worst driveline losses, because it drives through fluid. What it buys you is the launch: the converter stalls up against the brakes and multiplies engine torque (up to ~1.9× at a 2400 rpm stall), fading to 1:1 as the turbine catches up. That's why an automatic can out-launch a manual despite giving away power everywhere else — and why it suits a heavy, torquey car far better than a light, peaky one. It also spreads its ratios wider: a lower 1st to get the load moving, a taller top for cruising.
The TRACK tab puts the car on a circuit and solves a lap. First it works out the racing line: nobody drives the centreline, because using the full width of the track straightens a corner and so raises the speed you can carry through it. The line is found by minimising its total curvature inside the track edges, and out-in-out through corners, straightened esses and linked complexes all fall out of that on their own. Two things bend it away from pure minimum curvature. First, the track is not one width: every circuit here narrows through its tight sections and opens out on the fast ones, and a 7 m corner simply cannot be straightened as much as a 13 m one — the figure quoted on the map is the range, and the circuit's headline width is its average. Second, a late apex: a metre of radius at a corner exit is worth far more than a metre at the entry, because exit speed is carried the whole length of whatever follows while at the entry you are braking anyway. The solver prices curvature by how open the track is ahead, so it gives up entry radius to straighten the exit — moving the apex about 11 m later on corners that lead onto a straight, and leaving corners that lead into more corners exactly where they were. Circuits are three-dimensional, so the lap also accounts for elevation: gravity pulling you back up a climb and pushing you down a descent, the slightly reduced weight-on-tyres of a slope, and — the one that really bites — vertical curvature. Over a crest the car is thrown light and loses grip exactly where it is going fastest; through a compression it is pressed into the road and can carry far more speed. On the test circuits a dip is worth about a third more corner speed than the same corner on a brow. Where the hills fall matters as much as how big they are: a climb in a braking zone lets you brake later and gains time, while the same climb on a straight simply costs you.
IMPORT GPX TRACK loads a real circuit from a GPX trace — a recorded lap, an export from a mapping tool, anything with track points. The trace is projected to metres, resampled to an even spacing (GPX points are wildly uneven, and a starved spline invents corners) and lightly smoothed so GPS jitter doesn't read as curvature. Elevation comes straight from the file, so an imported circuit gets its real gradients. Imported tracks are marked * in the list and are kept in your browser only — nothing is uploaded, and nothing ships with the app.
On accuracy: an imported circuit's lap time will read a little slow. A GPX trace carries no width at all, so the width profile is modelled from the layout — roads are built wide where they are fast and narrow where they are tight — rather than surveyed. The line is still an approximation of the minimum-time line, and the driver never makes a mistake or takes a compromise line. Treat it as a consistent yardstick for comparing your own builds, not as a real lap record.
It is worth 5–7% of lap time over driving the centreline, and a wider circuit is worth more. Then it finds the cornering speed at every point (v = √(μ·(mg + downforce)·r / m)), then works backwards from each corner to find the braking points and forwards out of each corner on the available drive, taking the lower speed at every point. A friction circle ties the two together: grip already spent turning isn't available for accelerating or braking, so a car at the cornering limit can't also put its power down. The coloured line on the map shows what is limiting the car at each point, and the legend totals how much of the lap each one accounts for — a fast circuit is mostly power and braking, a tight one mostly traction out of slow corners. Shift time is charged per gear change around the lap, so a slow-shifting box loses real time.
One limit worth knowing: the line is still only an approximation of the minimum-time line. The late-apex bias is priced from the geometry ahead, not from the car's own speed, so the same line is driven by a 90 hp hatchback and an 800 hp aero car — where in reality the fast car would brake later and apex later still. It is worth about half a percent, and it is the same half percent for everyone, so build-to-build comparisons stay honest.
The differential splits drive between the two wheels on an axle — and decides how much of that axle's grip you can actually use. The two wheels are never loaded identically (road camber, engine torque reaction, axle wind-up, suspension geometry), and an open diff can only feed each wheel what the lighter-loaded one will hold: it spins that wheel and throws the rest away, leaving about 78% of the axle's grip usable at launch. That 78% is not a constant — most of the asymmetry it is fighting is torque reaction (driveshaft wind-up, anti-lift geometry, torque steer), which is proportional to the torque actually going through the axle. In first gear under full multiplication it is at its worst; in third at 100 km/h the axle sees a fraction of that torque and little more than road camber is left, so an open diff recovers toward ~94%. A viscous LSD ties the two together through fluid shear and recovers most of it (~92%); a clutch-plate LSD locks them against a preload for effectively all of it; a spool welds them solid and is the best of all in a straight line, which is why drag cars run one.
The key thing: a diff only matters while you're traction-limited. Fit a spool to a slow car and nothing changes — it never troubles its tyres. Fit one to a car that's spinning up off the line and it can be worth more than a power upgrade. Watch the LIMITED-BY behaviour: if your 0–100 barely moves when you change diff, the engine is the limit, not grip.
Why a front-driver runs out of grip so early. Three things multiply, and the first one surprises people: a FWD car only drives the front axle, which carries about 60% of the weight standing — and accelerating transfers weight off it, down to roughly 53%. Multiply that by an open diff (0.78) and by eco tyres (μ 0.81) and the whole car is capped at 0.33 g, whatever the engine makes. At 100 km/h that ceiling is about 2500 N, and even a 109 hp engine is already asking for 2700 N — so a 217 hp version of the same car reaches 100 km/h at exactly the same time. Both are just riding the ceiling. This is not a quirk of the simulation; it is why nobody builds a 200 hp front-drive kei car. The fix is grip, not power — on that same 217 hp car: a clutch LSD alone takes 9.9 s to 8.0 s, sport tyres 6.6 s, slicks 5.5 s, and switching to AWD 4.4 s. Same engine throughout.
And now the trade is measurable. On the TRACK tab a locked diff pays for its straight-line advantage: it scrubs in every corner (less lateral grip) even though it never wastes drive. On an 842 hp muscle car a spool is ~0.2 s quicker to 100 km/h than a clutch LSD and ~2.5 s slower per lap, while an open diff is worst at both — it spins the inside wheel hardest exactly when cornering has already unloaded it.
The gearing number — the 0–100 slider is your final drive ratio, how many times the driveshaft turns per turn of the wheels. It runs from 0 = 4.90:1 (the shortest, lowest gearing) to 100 = 2.60:1 (the tallest); the ratio is shown next to the number. Short gearing multiplies torque at the wheels, so the car accelerates harder and reaches the rev limiter sooner — quick 0–100, but each gear runs out of revs early. Tall gearing does the opposite: lazier acceleration, more speed per 1000 rpm and calmer cruising revs. Whatever you pick, top gear at the rev limiter is a hard ceiling — the limiter cuts fuel there, so no amount of power will push the car past the speed its own gearing allows, on the drag strip or on track. The sweet spot is the tallest gearing your engine can still pull to its power peak in top gear. Go taller than that and top speed simply stops improving — the car reaches its maximum a gear lower instead of bogging in top — while acceleration keeps getting worse, so you're paying for nothing. Big power and low drag can use very tall gearing and keep gaining speed all the way out; heavy, low-power or draggy cars plateau early and want shorter gearing.
The engine's mass and torque curve are added in and the Performance panel computes 0–100 km/h, 400/800/1000 m (time + trap speed), top speed, 100–0 braking and the 0–200–0 test live — a traction-limited launch and a tyre/brake-limited stop with fade, weight transfer, downforce, drag and rolling resistance. Sticky tyres, big brakes and AWD help most.
