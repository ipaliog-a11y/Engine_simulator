How the app works

LV1 · REAL LIFE
Build it, measure it, learn from it: the loop every engine shop runs.
A real engine is designed on paper, built, bolted to a dynamometer (a brake that measures how hard the engine pushes), tuned, and only then put in a car and driven on a track. Each step teaches you something the step before could not: a drawing cannot tell you where the engine knocks, and a dyno cannot tell you whether the car will be quick through a corner.
This app is that whole workshop in one page. You are the engineer: you choose every part, and the simulation plays the part of the dyno cell, the test driver and the stopwatch.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
1. ENGINE — design an engine (bottom end, air path, fuel & spark, cooling…), then press BUILD & DYNO. 2. DYNO reads the full power/torque curve and a graded scorecard. 3. LIVE is a running test bench: you set THROTTLE and LOAD and watch the engine respond in real time. You never set RPM directly — you drive it.
Then put it in a car on VEHICLE and take it to a circuit on TRACK. Every number in the app comes out of the same physics, so a change you make on the ENGINE tab shows up in the 0–100, the top speed and the lap time.
LV3 · THE PHYSICS
Every number in the app comes out of one chain of calculations. Nothing is looked up from a table of real cars; the chain starts from the parts you chose: geometry → breathing (VE) → air trapped → fuel burned → combustion work (IMEP)
− friction − pumping → BMEP → torque → wheel force → car → lap At each step the model uses a published law of physics or a measured engineering correlation, and says which in the code. The last link is the familiar one: torque is mean effective pressure acting on the swept volume, once every two turns on a four-stroke, T = BMEP · Vd / (4π) so a 2.0 L engine at 12.6 bar BMEP makes 200 Nm. The rest of this wiki walks that chain one link at a time. The pages in How an engine works are the place to start if the words are new; the LV3 boxes are where the equations live.

The tabs

LV1 · REAL LIFE
Design, test, drive, then back to the drawing board.
A car company is split into departments that hand the same project along: powertrain designs the engine, the test cell runs it on a dyno, vehicle dynamics fits it to a chassis, and the test track finds out whether all of it works together. The tabs are those departments.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
ENGINE — the design form: every part of the engine, a preset list to start from, an objective to aim at, and BUILD & DYNO.
VEHICLE — drop the engine into a chassis, choose tyres, gearbox, aero and brakes, and read the performance figures. The garage lives at the bottom.
TRACK — a solved lap of a real or imported circuit, with the racing line and what limits the car at every point.
LIVE — the engine running: a cutaway of one cylinder, the whole bank firing in order, and the controls on the right to drive it.
DYNO — the power and torque curves, the scorecard, and printable reports.
GUIDE — this manual. OPTIONS — sound, wear and language.
PARTS — design your own exhaust, intake manifold or air filter for the engine you built, preview it against what the engine has now, and keep it in a library that the ENGINE tab's lists offer under MY PARTS. See Designing your own parts.
RACE — two to eight cars on one circuit, run to a lap count, with tyres that heat and wear, fuel that burns off, pit stops and traffic. Nobody drives; you set it up and watch. See The race.
LV3 · THE PHYSICS
Each tab runs a different solver over the same engine, which is why they always agree with each other:
· ENGINE derives the hardware: valve sizes, valve float, pipe lengths, turbine throat, inertia, mass and cost (buildEngine).
· DYNO is a steady sweep: wide-open throttle every 100 rpm, each point solved to equilibrium (calcAtRpm).
· LIVE is a time simulation: manifold pressure, turbo shaft energy, crank speed, temperatures, battery and engine health are integrated step by step (stepEngine).
· VEHICLE integrates a standing start through the gearbox, and a stop (simulateAccel, simulateBraking).
· TRACK solves a lap: a racing line, then a speed at every metre of it (simulateLap).
· RACE solves each car's lap at several levels of grip and fuel, then runs them all together on a 50 ms clock (simulateRace).

Your first build

LV1 · REAL LIFE
Before and after one change. One change at a time is how you learn what it did.
Real engine development is slow on purpose. Engineers fix a baseline, change one thing, and measure again, because an engine is a web of trade-offs: a bigger cam makes power up top and costs it down low, more boost makes power and brings the engine closer to knock. Change three things at once and you cannot tell which one helped.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
Start from a preset. On the ENGINE tab, open the build list at the top and pick something close to what you want — a 1.6 sport four, a turbo hot hatch, a V8. Press BUILD & DYNO.
Read the DYNO tab. Note the peak power, where it arrives, and the grade. The scorecard tells you what is costing you: a low band score means the power is peaky, a low safety score means it is close to knocking.
Change one thing. Swap the cam, the runner length or the boost, build again and compare. One change at a time is the only way to learn what each part does — the LOG PULL button on the LIVE tab keeps the previous curve on the dyno as a faint overlay.
Drive it. On LIVE, raise the throttle and use LOAD to hold an rpm. Watch the knock bar, the charge temperature and the EGT; with WEAR on, abuse breaks things.
Put it in a car. On VEHICLE, pick a chassis. The final drive is matched to your engine automatically, so the 0–100 and top speed are fair from the start. Then try TRACK.
LV3 · THE PHYSICS
Why one change moves so much: the parts are coupled through the physics, not through a list of bonuses. A wilder cam alone changes the intake closing angle (so how much charge is trapped at low rpm), the valve overlap (so whether the exhaust can scavenge), the residual gas left behind (so the end-gas temperature and knock), the valve lift (so the valve-float rpm) and the idle quality. Each of those then feeds torque, EGT, emissions and the score. The DYNO overlay from LOG PULL shows the sum of all of it.

Driving controls

LV1 · REAL LIFE
A dyno is a brake you can read: it soaks up the engine's torque and measures it.
On a real engine dyno there is no road and no car. The engine drives a brake (a water brake or an eddy-current drum) and the operator has two levers: the throttle, which decides how much the engine tries, and the brake load, which decides how hard it is held back. Speed is what is left over: if the engine makes more torque than the brake takes, it speeds up; if less, it slows down. That is exactly how the LIVE tab drives.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
THROTTLE — opens the intake; raises manifold pressure → torque → revs.
LOAD (dyno brake) — resistance that sets the hold RPM. 0% = free-rev to the limiter; 100% = holds near idle. It only resists above its target, so any engine can always rev up to it.
ELEC LOAD — accessory draw (lights/AC). Heavy load + small alternator at idle drains the battery → low voltage → weak spark.
AFR — air-fuel ratio. Petrol: peak power near λ0.86 (rich), best economy near λ1.1 (lean). Diesel: full fuel at the smoke limit, λ1.2; leaner is less torque but less fuel per kWh.
IAT / COOLANT / OIL — charge & engine temps (auto in AUTO THERMAL mode).
NITROUS — if fitted, sprays at wide-open throttle from a bottle that depletes.
PEAK TORQUE · MAX POWER · ECO — three buttons that put the engine you built on a real operating point and let it settle there. MAX POWER opens the throttle fully and has the dyno brake hold the rpm where this engine makes its peak power, at the best-power mixture (λ0.86 petrol, λ1.2 diesel) and the scorecard's conditions — so the LIVE OUTPUT should land on the same figure the DYNO tab prints. PEAK TORQUE does the same at the torque peak. ECO searches this engine's speed, load and mixture for the point that burns the least fuel per kWh — the lowest BSFC — and holds it; for a petrol engine that is usually a little lean of stoich at high load and modest rpm, for a diesel well lean. Each button flashes what it set and what to expect. With the mixture on AUTO, the ECU's fuel map chooses the mixture instead.
LV3 · THE PHYSICS
The LIVE engine is an equation of motion for the crankshaft, integrated in small time steps: J · dω/dt = Tengine(N, pman) − Tbrake − Talternator J is the rotating inertia (crank, rods, pistons, flywheel and clutch, sized from the engine's own torque). Manifold pressure is a state too: it fills toward what the throttle and the turbo allow with a time constant set by the intake system, so a blip of throttle takes a moment to arrive. The dyno brake holds a target speed, Ntarget = redline − load × (redline − idle), and only resists above it, so the engine can always rev up to it. Lift off above idle and an injected engine cuts the fuel completely (DFCO): the torque goes negative, which is engine braking. At the limiter the fuel is cut the same way.

Watching it run

LV1 · REAL LIFE
A piston is not a sine wave. It dwells at the bottom and snaps through the top.
Cut a real engine in half and you can watch the parts that make it work: the piston sliding in its bore, the connecting rod swinging, the crank going round, the valves opening on the cam. Museums do exactly this with an electric motor turning the crank slowly. The LIVE tab is that cutaway, but of the engine you designed and running on the physics.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
The LIVE tab is a cutaway of the engine you just designed. Everything on it moves because the simulation moved it. It is deliberately not drawn in real time: a crank at 5000 rpm turns 83 times a second, which at 60 fps is a strobe rather than a picture — you could not watch a valve open. So the cycle runs at a pace you can read, between about 3.3 s and 1.1 s per full four-stroke cycle, still ranked by rpm so idle is visibly lazier than the limiter. The true speed is on the tachometer above it and in the note; the animation's job is to make the cycle legible. The piston follows proper crank-slider geometry (not a sine wave — a real rod of finite length makes the piston linger at the bottom and snap through the top), the bore is drawn to your actual bore/stroke ratio so an oversquare engine really is short and wide, and the valves open on true four-stroke timing with the scavenging overlap visible around TDC. The chamber is colour-coded by what is in it right now — fresh charge, compressed charge, burning, burnt — and the stroke is named above the head, so the cycle is something you can watch rather than something you have to remember. A two-stroke shows ports uncovering in the liner instead of valves; a rotary shows a real epitrochoid housing with the rotor turning at a third of the eccentric shaft. The strip beside it draws every cylinder at its own phase, so you can see the firing order run through a V12.
The Wankel view. A rotary is drawn with the same instruments as a piston engine — throttle, turbo, exhaust and EGT, breathing, output, fuel and spark, and a rotor bank with each rotor numbered and lit as it fires. The housing is a true two-lobe epitrochoid. A Wankel has two "top dead centres", at the two waists of the housing: the ports sit at one and the spark plugs at the other. Each of the three chambers is coloured by what is in it, and unlike a piston engine every phase happens at a fixed place — intake in one lobe, power in the other — which is labelled on the drawing.
The numbers that used to be printed here in 7 px type now live in the LIVE OUTPUT panel beside the canvas, where they are actual text; what stays on the cutaway is what benefits from being next to the part it describes — throttle plate angle, boost and compressor flow at the turbo, EGT at the exhaust, and the knock bar.
LV3 · THE PHYSICS
The piston follows the crank-slider: crank radius r (half the stroke) and connecting rod l (1.7 stroke in this model), x(θ) = r(1 − cos θ) + l − √(l² − r² sin² θ) which is a sine wave plus a second harmonic of size r/l. That second harmonic is why the piston spends longer near the bottom than the top, why an inline four shakes at twice crank speed, and why the trapped volume at a late intake-valve closing is bigger than a sine wave suggests. The same function sizes the cylinder volume everywhere in the model, from the trapped charge to the knock trace.

Reading the numbers

LV1 · REAL LIFE
Instruments, not decoration: every one of them is a number the simulation computed.
A dyno operator reads a handful of numbers off the screen and each one answers a question. Torque: how hard does it push? Power: how fast can it do work? BMEP: is that a lot for its size? VE: how well does it breathe? BSFC: how thirsty is it? EGT and knock: is it about to hurt itself?
LV2 · IN THE GAME
HP / kW — power = torque × rpm. TORQUE (Nm) — twisting force; peaks lower than power.
BMEP (bar) — brake mean effective pressure: torque normalised to displacement, so you can compare engines of any size. VE (%) — volumetric efficiency: how well the engine breathes vs its swept volume (boost can exceed 100%).
THERMAL EFF (%) — share of fuel energy turned into work. BSFC (g/kWh) — fuel burned per unit power; lower is better (best around stoich, part-load).
FUEL FLOW (L/h) — live consumption. MAP / BOOST (bar) — manifold pressure; boost is MAP above 1.0.
KNOCK (LOW/MED/HIGH) — detonation risk. AUTO timing retards to control it; sustained HIGH does damage. HEALTH (%) — condition; falls under abuse, and 0 = a blown engine (rebuild).
EMISSIONS (CLEAN/OK/DIRTY) — from the exhaust analyser: CO & HC climb rich, NOx climbs lean/hot; a catalytic converter only cleans all three near stoich.
λ (lambda) — AFR ÷ the fuel's stoichiometric AFR: λ<1 rich, 1 = stoich, >1 lean.
COST ($) — total build price of the parts you chose. REL (reliability) — a design estimate of how much abuse the build invites (boost/knock/heat/high-rpm stress); higher is tougher. hp/$1k — power per thousand dollars: the value metric.
DYNO RESULT — under the chart on the DYNO tab, the pull as a static sheet: peak power and torque with their rpm, in hp, kW, Nm and lb-ft, and a run-data table. Two pulls are shown, because they answer two different questions. The best-power pull is the engine at its best — full throttle, best-power mixture, standard conditions — and is what the grade and the objectives use. Your pull is the curve drawn on the chart: the engine as you have it tuned right now. The PDF report tabulates the same pull.
LV3 · THE PHYSICS
The definitions the readouts use, with N in rpm, Vd the displacement: P = T · 2πN/60    (1 hp = 0.7457 kW)
BMEP = 4π · T / Vd    (four-stroke; 2π for a two-stroke or rotary)
VE = mair, trapped / (ρref · Vd)
BSFC = ṁfuel / P    η = 3600 / (BSFC[g/kWh] · LHV[MJ/kg]) So 250 g/kWh on petrol (LHV 44 MJ/kg) is 32.7% efficient, and 200 g/kWh on diesel (42.8) is 42%. Lambda is the mixture against the chemically exact one: λ = AFR / AFRstoich.

The four strokes

LV1 · REAL LIFE
Suck, squeeze, bang, blow: two turns of the crank, one power stroke.
Almost every car engine repeats four strokes, two turns of the crankshaft:
1. Intake (suck): the piston goes down with the intake valve open and draws in air and fuel.
2. Compression (squeeze): both valves shut, the piston goes up and squeezes the charge to about a tenth of its volume.
3. Power (bang): a spark lights it, the gas burns, gets very hot, its pressure soars and it shoves the piston down. This is the only stroke that does work.
4. Exhaust (blow): the exhaust valve opens and the piston pushes the burnt gas out.
Nikolaus Otto built the first one that worked in 1876, and the cycle still carries his name.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
The LIVE tab draws exactly this cycle for your engine: the chamber is coloured by what is in it (fresh charge, compressed charge, burning, burnt) and the stroke is named above the head. Two-strokes and rotaries do the same four jobs differently, see Engine types.
LV3 · THE PHYSICS
The ideal version is the Otto cycle: compression and expansion without heat loss, and burning at constant volume at the top. Its efficiency depends only on the compression ratio rc: ηOtto = 1 − rc−(γ−1) With cold air (γ = 1.4) and 10:1 that is 60%, which no engine reaches. Burning gas is not cold air: it dissociates and its γ falls, so the fuel-air cycle with an effective γ of about 1.28 gives 47% at 10:1 (Heywood, fig. 5-9). A real engine reaches about 0.8 of that, because the burn takes time and the walls take heat. That is what this model uses for a spark engine: IMEPgross = η · (mair / AFRst) · LHV · f(λ) / Vd,   η ≈ 0.8 · ηfuel-air The area inside the pressure-volume loop below is the work of one cycle.

Air, fuel and spark

LV1 · REAL LIFE
Fire needs three things. An engine controls all three, thousands of times a minute.
Burning needs fuel, oxygen and something to light it. In an engine the oxygen comes from air, and the amount of air is what really limits power: there is only so much air a cylinder can swallow, and you can only burn as much fuel as that air has oxygen for. For petrol the chemically exact mix is 14.7 kg of air for every kg of fuel, so one litre of petrol needs about 9 cubic metres of air.
A mixture with extra fuel is rich; with extra air it is lean. Rich runs cooler and makes a little more power; lean saves fuel but burns hotter and, too lean, misfires.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
The AFR slider sets the mixture when the fuel mode is MANUAL, and the λ readout shows it against the fuel's own stoichiometric ratio. On AUTO the ECU fuel map chooses it for every load and rpm, see Fuel, injection & the fuel map. Choose the fuel itself on the ENGINE tab: alcohols carry far less energy per kilogram but need far less air to burn it.
LV3 · THE PHYSICS
Heat is released only by fuel that finds oxygen, so above stoichiometric the release is capped and the extra fuel leaves as CO and hydrogen. Work per unit of fuel energy follows the fuel-air cycle tables (Heywood): lean of stoich the efficiency rises (about +12% from φ = 1.0 to 0.8, where φ = 1/λ), rich it falls (−14% at φ 1.2). Torque goes as φ · η(φ), which peaks near λ 0.83, about 3% above stoich, the shape a real spark engine shows. Past λ 1.12 the burn deteriorates (lean limit) and below 0.75 it misfires.

Torque vs power

LV1 · REAL LIFE
Torque is the twist. Power is twist times how fast you turn.
Torque is a twisting force: a 1 metre wrench with 100 N on the end puts 100 Nm on the bolt. Power is how fast work gets done: the same twist turned twice as fast is twice the power. A cyclist feels the difference: pushing hard on the pedals at low cadence is torque; spinning fast in a low gear is how you make power for a climb.
Gears turn one into the other. A low gear multiplies the engine's torque at the wheels and divides its speed, so what the car feels is the engine's power. That is why racing engines rev: power = torque × rpm, and revs are the cheap way to make it.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
The DYNO tab draws both curves. Peak torque arrives where the engine breathes best per cycle; peak power arrives later, because rpm keeps climbing while torque only gently falls. A wide gap between the two peaks, and a flat torque curve, make a car easy to drive: the scorecard rates that as band and drive.
LV3 · THE PHYSICS
P = T · ω = T · 2πN/60    dP/dN = 0  ⇔  dT/dN = −T/N Power peaks where torque is falling as fast, in proportion, as rpm rises. At the wheels, through the total ratio i with efficiency η, the drive force on a wheel of radius r is F = T · i · η / r    v = ω · r / i  ⇒  F · v = T · ω · η Gearing cancels out of the product: at any road speed the most force you can have is the most power the engine can make at some rpm, divided by speed. That is why the car is geared to keep the engine near its power peak.

Why engines have a redline

LV1 · REAL LIFE
Four separate things stop an engine revving. Whichever comes first is the redline.
Spin an engine faster and it makes more power, until something gives. Four things gang up on it:
· Piston speed: the piston stops and starts twice every turn, and the forces to do that grow with the square of rpm. Rods stretch, bearings fail.
· Valve float: the spring that shuts each valve can only pull it back so fast. Past that the valve stops following the cam and can meet the piston.
· Breathing: the air has less and less time to get in, and the valves start to choke it.
· Burn time: the flame needs time to cross the chamber. A diesel's slow burn is why it revs so little.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
The ENGINE tab shows the valve float rpm next to the springs, in red if it is below your redline. A rev limiter cuts fuel at the redline you set; with WEAR on, sitting at it wears the engine (OVER-REV). Pick a short stroke, light valves and stiff springs to rev high, see Bore, stroke & displacement and Valvetrain & cams.
LV3 · THE PHYSICS
mean piston speed Sp = 2 · S · N / 60 Road engines sit at 15–25 m/s at the limiter, race engines near 30. The valve stays on the cam while the spring force at full lift exceeds the valve's inertia at the nose of the lobe: meff · acam · (L/2) · (2π/θcam)² · ωcam² ≤ Fseat + k·L solved for rpm. Breathing is Taylor's inlet Mach index Z = (Apiston/Avalve) · Sp / (Ci · c): volumetric efficiency holds to about Z = 0.5 and falls away past it. Burn time is in Combustion & the burn.

Engine types

LV1 · REAL LIFE
Four ways to get a power stroke: petrol, diesel, two-stroke and the Wankel rotary.
Petrol engines (Otto, 1876) squeeze a mixture of air and fuel and light it with a spark. Diesels (Rudolf Diesel, 1893) squeeze only air, so hard it gets hot enough to light fuel sprayed straight into it: no spark at all. Two-strokes, in chainsaws, scooters and old rally cars, fire every turn instead of every other, using holes in the cylinder wall instead of valves. The Wankel rotary, made famous by Mazda's RX-7 and RX-8, replaces the piston with a triangular rotor turning in an oval housing.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
Petrol 4-stroke — the all-rounder: spark ignition, one power stroke every two revs.
Diesel (CI) — compression ignition (no spark, no spark-knock), very high compression, runs lean and unthrottled. Big low-end torque, low redline, and the best efficiency — but heavy, and its emissions signature is NOx + soot (PM), cleaned by a DPF/SCR system.
2-stroke — a power stroke every rev (ported, no valvetrain): high specific output for its size but thirsty and HC-dirty (it burns oil and short-circuits fresh charge).
Rotary (Wankel) — a spinning rotor firing every rev: turbine-smooth, revs sky-high, high power per litre, but thirsty, HC-heavy and hard on apex seals (lower reliability).
Switching type retargets the compression, redline and AFR ranges and disables what doesn't apply (spark for diesel; cam/VVT for ported engines).
LV3 · THE PHYSICS
The cycle sets how many power strokes a turn: T = BMEP · Vd / (4π) for a four-stroke and /(2π) for a two-stroke or rotary, which is most of why those two make so much power for their size.
A diesel is quality-governed: its throttle is the amount of fuel, never the air. Its torque is computed from the fuel it burns, IMEP = ηgross · mfuel · LHV / Vd, with ηgross 0.46 for a passenger-car direct-injection diesel at 17:1 and λ 1.2 (from production engines at 200–210 g/kWh), corrected for compression, mixture and burn timing. It is fuelled no richer than the smoke limit, λ 1.20, and its burn takes a fixed time, see Combustion & the burn.
A two-stroke loses about 30% of the charge it delivers straight out of the exhaust port while both are open, so it burns 1.45 times the fuel it traps.
A rotary's flame has to cross a long, thin, moving chamber: 130° of eccentric shaft with its burn centred 37.5° after top centre, against about 50° and 8° in a piston engine. On its own volume curve that phasing earns 0.86 of the ideal expansion work against the piston's 0.96, and the work not done goes out of the exhaust port as heat.
Limit: the two-stroke's and the rotary's work still rest on measured type factors (0.60 and 0.70 of a four-stroke's IMEP) and a fitted breathing curve; only the four-stroke cycles are fully derived. See Known limits.

Bore, stroke & displacement

LV1 · REAL LIFE
Same idea, two shapes: a wide, short engine revs; a narrow, long one pulls.
Displacement (the "2.0" in a 2.0 litre) is the volume the pistons sweep: bore area × stroke × number of cylinders. A Formula 1 engine of the V10 era had a bore nearly twice its stroke (oversquare) and revved to 19,000 rpm; a truck diesel has a stroke longer than its bore (undersquare) and pulls from idle.
Compression ratio is how hard the charge is squeezed: the volume with the piston at the bottom divided by the volume at the top. Higher squeezes more work out of the same fuel, until the charge gets so hot it explodes on its own: that is knock.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
Bottom end — engine type, bore × stroke × cylinders = displacement; oversquare & higher redline shift the powerband up.
Bore and stroke. Displacement is bore area × stroke × cylinders, but the shape matters as much as the size. A short stroke (oversquare) means the piston travels less per revolution, so the engine can rev higher before the piston speed becomes dangerous, and a wide bore leaves room for bigger valves. A long stroke (undersquare) gives more leverage and fills well at low rpm.
Mean piston speed is the limit that actually stops an engine revving: 2 × stroke × rpm / 60. Road engines sit at 15–25 m/s at the limiter and race engines reach about 30. Set a redline far past that and the reciprocating parts are no longer a road engine.
Compression ratio sets thermal efficiency — a higher ratio extracts more work from the same fuel — and it sets knock: the more the charge is squeezed, the hotter the end gas and the closer it is to detonating. An NA petrol engine runs 10.5–12.5:1, a turbo engine 8.5–10:1, a diesel 15–20:1.
Layout — inline, V or boxer — decides how the cylinders are arranged and, with the cylinder count, the firing order you see on the LIVE tab. A V or a boxer runs one exhaust system per bank.
LV3 · THE PHYSICS
Vd = (π/4) · B² · S · ncyl    rc = (Vswept + Vc) / Vc Friction grows with speed and with stroke. Crank & firing order

LV1 · REAL LIFE
An inline four: 1 and 4 move together, 2 and 3 together, and they fire 1-3-4-2.
The crankshaft turns the pistons' up-and-down into rotation, and its shape fixes when each cylinder can fire. Engineers spread the firings evenly so the engine runs smoothly, and choose the order so neighbouring cylinders do not fire one after another (that would load one part of the crank and starve one part of the intake).
Some engines are naturally smooth: an inline six and a V12 cancel all their shaking forces. An inline four buzzes at twice engine speed, which is why bigger fours carry balance shafts. The classic American cross-plane V8 fires its two banks unevenly, and that uneven beat is its famous burble.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
The bank, and the firing order. The panel on the right of the cutaway draws every cylinder, numbered, at its own point in the cycle. The number over a cylinder lights up while that cylinder fires, so you can read the order straight off the drawing, and the caption underneath spells it out.
Cylinders do not fire in the order they are numbered. An inline four fires 1-3-4-2, which is also why pistons 1 and 4 are always at the top together and 2 and 3 at the bottom: the firing order and the crank geometry are the same fact. An inline six fires 1-5-3-6-2-4, a V8 1-5-4-8-6-3-7-2, a V12 1-7-5-11-3-9-6-12-2-8-4-10.
V and boxer engines are numbered bank by bank — bank A is 1 to n/2 on the top row, bank B the rest below — which is how BMW, Audi, Ferrari and Porsche number theirs, and the orders above are in that numbering. (GM numbers a V8 odd-left, even-right, so its familiar 1-8-4-3-6-5-7-2 is the same engine written differently.) Counts with no production layout, such as a V5, fire in sequence, which is still evenly spaced.
LV3 · THE PHYSICS
On a four-stroke every cylinder fires once in 720° of crank, so evenly spaced firings are 720°/n apart: 180° for a four, 120° for a six, 90° for a V8. The crank-slider's second harmonic (r/l on Watching it run) is why a flat four-cylinder crank leaves an unbalanced shaking force at twice crank speed, even though the pistons are paired.
The firing order is used for real in two places: the LIVE bank draws each cylinder at its own phase, and the engine sound fires each cylinder's exhaust pulse in that order down its own bank's pipe, so a cross-plane V8's A-B-A-B-B-A-B-A bank sequence produces its beat without anything in the code knowing what a V8 sounds like.
Not modelled: vibration and balance. An engine's shaking forces do not cost it power or reliability here.

Valvetrain & cams

LV1 · REAL LIFE
The cam's bump lifts the valve; the spring brings it back. Lift against crank angle is the cam's whole personality.
Valves are the engine's doors. A camshaft with an egg-shaped lobe for each valve pushes it open, and a stiff spring snaps it shut. The lobe's shape decides how far the valve opens (lift), for how long (duration), and how long the intake and exhaust valves are open together around top dead centre (overlap).
A "big" race cam opens the valves wider and longer: at high rpm the air has momentum and keeps flowing in, so the engine breathes better up top. At low rpm the same cam lets charge flow back out, so it idles lumpy and feels weak down low. Four valves per cylinder instead of two give more door area in the same bore.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
Valvetrain — wilder cams move power up top & idle lumpy; VVT restores the bottom end. Valves per cylinder, valve material and springs are now modelled from real geometry rather than assumed. Four valves flow about 22% more than two for the same bore (and five flow no more than four — which is why nobody builds them any more). The app derives the valve diameter your bore can physically fit, works out what that valve weighs, and tells you the valve float rpm — the speed at which the spring can no longer pull the valve back down and it stops following the cam. If that number is below your redline, the engine cannot use the rev range it claims. Fit stiffer springs, lighter titanium valves, less lift, or lower the redline. Big-bore two-valve engines also choke on inlet airflow near the limiter, which is the real reason a big pushrod V8 makes its power low and a four-valve screamer can rev.

Variable valve timing

LV1 · REAL LIFE
A phaser slides the whole intake lobe earlier or later, moving the overlap and the closing point.
A fixed cam is a compromise between low-rpm manners and high-rpm power. Since the 1990s most engines carry a cam phaser (BMW's VANOS, Toyota's VVT-i): an oil-driven coupling that rotates the camshaft against its sprocket, so the ECU can move the valve timing while the engine runs. Honda's VTEC goes further and switches to a second, wilder lobe.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
Set VARIABLE VALVE TIMING to VVT on the ENGINE tab. It is worth most to a big cam: it brings back the bottom end the cam took away without giving up the top.
LV3 · THE PHYSICS
The phaser here can advance the intake lobe by up to 45 crank degrees, which moves the intake closing point by the full amount. At low rpm the charge has time to be pushed back out before the valve shuts, so what is trapped is the cylinder volume at IVC, not at BDC: trapped fraction ≈ V(IVC) / V(BDC)   (from the crank-slider) At high rpm the air column's momentum keeps filling the cylinder after BDC. The model integrates that intake stroke for every engine (see Intake runner waves) and tabulates the fill at every speed for every phaser position, then runs, at full load, the phase that fills best: what an ECU's wide-open-throttle calibration does.

Induction & throttle

LV1 · REAL LIFE
The throttle is a flap in the pipe. Closing it starves the engine of air, which is how you make it do less.
A petrol engine is controlled by starving it of air. The throttle, a butterfly flap in the intake, closes to choke the airflow; the pressure in the manifold behind it falls, each cylinder fills with thinner air, and power drops. Floor the pedal and the flap stands edge-on.
How fuel is added has changed over the decades: a carburettor uses the suction of the air through a venturi to draw fuel in; fuel injection (EFI) squirts a metered amount under computer control; individual throttle bodies (ITBs) give every cylinder its own flap, for instant response and a race-car intake howl.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
Induction — carbs → EFI → ITBs set breathing, metering precision and idle quality.
LV3 · THE PHYSICS
Manifold pressure is a state with a time constant: it fills toward the target set by throttle, rpm and boost at a rate set by the intake system's response (ITBs fastest, small carburettors slowest). Throttling costs pumping work, charged as PMEPintake = 0.9 bar × (1 − pman) at a closed throttle, which is engine braking. The metering accuracy of each system scales the fuel actually needed (a carburettor or mechanical injection delivers about 7–12% more fuel than the engine needs).
Limit: the induction systems' flow and response factors are hand-set to real relative behaviour, not derived from their geometry. The air filter's restriction is derived (build 210): its media by Darcy's law, its housing by its velocity head (Designing your own parts).

Fuel, injection & the fuel map

LV1 · REAL LIFE
An injector sprays fuel; the ECU's map decides how much at every load and rpm.
A modern engine's computer, the ECU, looks up how much fuel to inject from a map: a table with engine speed along one side and load along the other. At a light-load cruise it aims at exactly stoichiometric, where the catalyst works and fuel is not wasted. At full load it adds fuel: a richer mixture makes a little more power and, more importantly, cools the exhaust and the pistons.
Port injection sprays onto the back of the intake valve. Direct injection sprays into the cylinder itself, where the evaporating fuel cools the charge and makes knock less likely.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
Fuel & spark — fuel type, octane, injector, ignition type, FIXED vs AUTO (ECU) timing, and the fuel mixture: MANUAL (you set one AFR) or AUTO, a 3D ECU map that runs near stoich at light load and enriches under load/boost for power — just like a real fuel map. In AUTO you can tune the map: tap any cell to select it, then − LEAN / + RICH to shift that load×rpm target (RESET MAP restores the factory grid).
LV3 · THE PHYSICS
The factory map in this model, for a spark engine, as λ: λ = 1.02 − 0.16 · load − 0.07 · (boost/1.2) − 0.015 · (near redline),   0.78 ≤ λ ≤ 1.05 so about λ 1.0 at cruise and 0.86 at wide-open throttle, richer under boost to hold the exhaust temperature. A diesel's map runs from λ 3.4 at light load to the 1.20 smoke limit at full pedal, at whatever air it has at that speed.
Direct injection evaporates the fuel in the cylinder, taking its latent heat from the charge: ΔT = hfg · (F/A) / cp, with hfg 350 kJ/kg for petrol. Alcohols carry much more cooling (E85 15 °C, methanol 25 °C in this model), which is a large part of their knock resistance.

Ignition & spark timing

LV1 · REAL LIFE
The spark fires before the top. The flame takes time to grow, so the pressure peaks just after.
A spark plug lights the mixture, but the flame needs time to spread across the chamber, a millisecond or two. So the spark is fired before the piston reaches the top (spark advance), timed so the pressure peaks just after top dead centre, when it can push the piston down hardest. Too little advance wastes the burn on a falling piston; too much fights the piston on its way up and invites knock.
The ignition system also has to recharge its coil between sparks. An old distributor with one coil runs out of time at high rpm and misfires; coil-on-plug gives every cylinder its own coil.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
IGNITION CONTROL FIXED runs the advance you set; AUTO lets the ECU find the best timing at every point and pull it back when the engine is about to knock (watch the knock bar and the power stop following the boost). IGNITION TYPE sets where the spark starts to fail: about 5200 rpm for a distributor, 6800 for wasted spark, 9000 for coil-on-plug, sooner when the battery voltage sags (see Electrical & charging).
LV3 · THE PHYSICS
The best timing, MBT (minimum advance for best torque), puts the 50% burn point (CA50) about 8° after top centre. The model knows how much work any other phasing gives from the cycle itself: the Wiebe burn (see Combustion & the burn) is placed at the CA50 the spark gives, and each bit of heat is worth the expansion still ahead of it. Every degree of advance moves CA50 by a degree.
On AUTO the ECU runs MBT where the end gas survives it, and otherwise the most advance at which it just survives: the knock limit, never more than 37° of retard past best phasing. That search uses the same end-gas chemistry as Compression & knock.
Limit: the MBT advance itself is a simple rule, 18° + 14° × (rpm/redline) − 6° per bar of boost, clamped to 8–40°.

Combustion & the burn

LV1 · REAL LIFE
The Wiebe function: how much of the charge has burned at each crank angle, and how fast.
Combustion is not an explosion. In a petrol engine a flame front grows from the spark and sweeps across the chamber, carried along by the swirling, tumbling air. That turbulence grows with engine speed, so the burn takes about the same number of crank degrees at any rpm, around 50°.
A diesel is different: its fuel burns as fast as the injector can deliver it and the spray can find air, which is a fixed time. At high rpm that time becomes a lot of crank degrees, the heat arrives late in the stroke and does less work. That, not breathing, is why diesels run out of breath at 4500 rpm.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
You see combustion indirectly: in the torque curve's shape, in EGT (heat that did not become work leaves through the exhaust), and in the knock bar. The LIVE cutaway colours the chamber while it burns.
LV3 · THE PHYSICS
The burned fraction follows Wiebe's function (Heywood's a = 5, m = 2): xb(θ) = 1 − exp(−a · ((θ − θ0)/Δθ)m+1) with Δθ = 50° for a spark engine, 130° for a rotary, and a fixed 2.5 ms for a diesel (30° at 2000 rpm, 72° at 4800). Each increment of heat earns the Otto efficiency of the expansion still ahead of it: η(θ) = 1 − (V(θ)/VBDC)γ−1 and the cycle's efficiency is that, weighted by the burn rate. Whatever is not turned into work stays in the gas and leaves through the exhaust, so a late burn shows up as hotter EGT, which in a turbo diesel is what finally limits the fuel (the ECU will not push the gas at the turbine past 1100 K).

Heat, cooling & EGT

LV1 · REAL LIFE
Where the fuel's energy goes at full load: roughly a third becomes work.
Only about a third of the fuel's energy turns the crank. Roughly another third leaves as hot exhaust (often 800–950 °C on a petrol engine at full power), and most of the rest soaks into the cylinder walls, the coolant and the oil. The cooling system carries that heat to the radiator, where the air blowing through takes it away; a thermostat keeps the engine shut off from the radiator until it is warm; a fan pulls air through when the car is slow.
The radiator and its fan: the coolant gives its heat to the air.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
Cooling & electrical — radiator/fan/oil-cooler/thermostat set temps; alternator size and ignition type set charging and the misfire RPM limit.
With AUTO THERMAL on, coolant and oil temperatures drift with load toward a level set by the radiator, fan and thermostat. An under-cooled build heat-soaks: watch COOLANT climb, and with WEAR on, OVERHEAT and OIL TEMP start eating the engine's health. The EGT readout is the exhaust gas temperature, computed from the energy balance below.
LV3 · THE PHYSICS
For poppet-valve engines the exhaust's share is an energy balance, closed: Qexhaust = Qfuel − Wgross − Qwalls − Qport The wall heat comes from Woschni's correlation integrated over a traced cycle, h ∝ B−0.2 p0.8 T−0.55 w0.8, with the gas velocity w from piston speed plus a combustion term and the walls at 450 K. The exhaust port's share uses Caton & Heywood's measured Nu = 0.0483 Re0.783. A joule lost to the wall early in expansion would have done work; one lost late would only have gone out of the exhaust, and the model splits it that way, so energy is conserved by construction.
EGT is shown two ways in the model: the mass-averaged temperature the turbine works on, and what a thermocouple in the pipe would read (closer to the time average, a little cooler, because the hottest gas passes quickest).
Limit: the coolant and oil temperatures come from a lumped model (a heat load against a cooling capacity, with first-order lags), not from heat flows through the block.

Friction, oil & pumping

LV1 · REAL LIFE
Rings scraping, bearings turning and air being pushed around: all of it costs work.
Some of the work the gas does on the pistons never reaches the flywheel. Friction takes it: piston rings scraping the bore, bearings turning in oil, the valvetrain, the oil and water pumps. And the engine has to pump air: suck it past a part-closed throttle and push the exhaust out through the pipes. At light load that pumping is a big share of the fuel a car burns, which is why a diesel (no throttle) is so frugal in traffic.
Close the throttle while moving and those losses slow the car: that is engine braking.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
You see friction as the gap between what the cylinders make and what the crank delivers, and in how fast the engine slows when you lift. Cold, thick oil adds friction: a cold engine is down on power until it warms.
LV3 · THE PHYSICS
The mean effective pressures are added up and taken off the gross work: BMEP = IMEPgross − FMEP − PMEP
FMEP = (0.55 + 1.8·10−8 N²) · koil · kstroke + alternator + blower drive
PMEP = (pexhaust − 1) + 0.9 · (1 − pmanifold)   [bar] The exhaust half is derived from the back-pressure the pipes and turbine actually make (see Back-pressure & residuals); the intake half is the throttling. A diesel's loop is charged with its sign, so a turbo that makes more boost than back-pressure gives work back. The alternator's drag is the electrical power it is making divided by 0.55 efficiency, over crank speed: large at idle, negligible at the top.
Limit: the friction curve is a standard correlation shape, not built from ring, bearing and valvetrain friction separately.

Electrical & charging

LV1 · REAL LIFE
The crank drives the alternator; the alternator charges the battery; the battery feeds the spark.
The engine makes its own electricity. A belt from the crank spins an alternator, which charges the battery and runs everything electrical: the ECU, the fuel pump, the lights, the fan. At idle a small alternator cannot keep up with a big electrical load, the battery drains, the voltage sags, and a weak spark starts to misfire.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
ELEC LOAD on the LIVE tab is the accessories (lights, air conditioning, audio). Pick the ALTERNATOR size on the ENGINE tab: 60, 120 or 180 A. Watch the battery voltage at idle with the load turned up.
LV3 · THE PHYSICS
The alternator gives about 35% of its rating at idle, rising to full output by 2500 rpm. Demand is a 12 A baseline (ECU, pump, ignition), up to 55 A of accessories and 22 A for an electric fan when the coolant is hot. The battery integrates the difference, and system voltage follows its state of charge. The spark's strength scales with voltage between 9.5 and 13 V, and the ignition's dwell limit falls with it, so a flat battery and a distributor misfire early. The power drawn costs crank torque: Talt = I · 14 V / 0.55 / ω

The air path

LV1 · REAL LIFE
Filter, throttle, runner, valve, cylinder, header, catalyst: every metre of it decides how much air gets in.
An engine is an air pump that happens to burn fuel. Fuel is easy to add; air is the hard part, because you can only burn as much fuel as you have air for. So nearly every way of making more power is really a way of getting more air through: bigger valves, tuned pipes, more revs, or a compressor to force it in.
Volumetric efficiency (VE) is how full the cylinder gets compared with its own volume of outside air. A good naturally aspirated engine reaches 90–100% at its torque peak, and tuned pipes can push past 100%.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
Air path — turbo (spools with rpm; size/config trade lag vs top-end) or supercharger (boost from idle); intercooler size cools the charge (denser, less knock) with a little lag; exhaust and catalytic converter trade flow vs back-pressure/emissions; nitrous is bottled power.
LV3 · THE PHYSICS
The air an engine swallows per second, four-stroke, with ρ = p/(R·T) the density in the manifold: ṁair = ρman · VE · Vd · N / (2 · 60) For a poppet-valve engine VE is not a fitted curve: it is the product of what the model derives, the fill of the integrated intake stroke (with the charge's momentum, heating and the cam's closing point), the exhaust's scavenging or reversion, residual gas, inlet Mach choking (Taylor), valve float, the induction system and filter, and for a turbo the compressor's choke flow. Intake runner waves

LV1 · REAL LIFE
Blow across a bottle and it hums at one note. An intake runner and cylinder hum the same way.
Blow across the top of a bottle and it sings: the air in the neck bounces on the springy air inside. An engine's intake runner and cylinder form the same kind of resonator. Every time an intake valve opens and shuts it sets the air in the runner swinging, and if the runner's length is right, a pressure peak arrives at the valve just as it is about to close, cramming in extra air. The engine is briefly supercharged by its own plumbing.
Long runners ring slowly and help at low rpm; short ones help up top. A variable intake (BMW's DISA, Porsche's VarioRam) switches between two lengths with a flap.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
An engine is an air pump, and most of what separates a good one from a bad one happens in the pipes rather than the cylinder. Three things here are solved rather than assumed, and all three are yours to tune.
The intake runner is a tuned organ pipe. Every time a valve slams shut the column of air behind it bounces, and a well-chosen runner length puts that returning pressure wave at the valve just as it opens again — the engine is briefly supercharged by its own plumbing. Long runners tune low (torque down low), short runners tune high (power up top), and the VARIABLE setting is simply two lengths and a flap, which is all a DISA or a VarioRam ever was. Worth up to about 20–25% of airflow at the tuned speed, and the reason a torque curve has humps rather than being a smooth arc.
LV3 · THE PHYSICS
The tract is a Helmholtz resonator: runner area A, length L, and the cylinder volume V as the spring, fH = (c / 2π) · √(A / (L · V)) Engelman's tuned-intake experiments put the main ram peak where fH / (N/60) ≈ 2, and the model uses that published ratio. More than that, it integrates the intake stroke itself as a lumped column of air between the plenum and the cylinder: ρL · du/dt = p0 − pcyl − ½ρ u|u| (Ar/Av)² − R·u    dm/dt = ρ Ar u with the valve curtain opening on the cam's lift curve, the cylinder heated by the hot residual and the walls (first law), and the damping set to the Q of about 4 a flow rig measures. The column's momentum is what keeps air flowing in after BDC, which is why a late-closing race cam makes power high. Pressure is converted to trapped mass isentropically, Δρ/ρ = (1 + Δp/p)1/γ − 1, so a +35% pressure spike is not +35% VE.

Exhaust headers & scavenging

LV1 · REAL LIFE
The blowdown pulse runs down the pipe, hits the collector and comes back as suction.
When the exhaust valve opens, burnt gas bursts out as a pressure pulse that races down the pipe. Where the pipe opens into a bigger one (the collector) the pulse reflects back as a suction wave. If that suction arrives while both valves are open at the top of the stroke, it pulls the last of the burnt gas out and drags fresh charge in behind it: scavenging.
That is why race headers are long, equal-length, carefully bent tubes: each one is cut so its echo arrives on time at the rpm the engine is built for.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
The exhaust header does the opposite job. The blowdown pulse races down the primary, hits the collector and reflects back as a rarefaction — a suction wave. Time it to arrive during valve overlap and it pulls the last of the burnt gas out and fresh charge in behind it. Primary length sets the arrival time, which is why headers are cut to a target rpm. The app sizes them from the engine's own flow rather than from a table.
The ENGINE tab shows what your header came out as: primary length, tuning order and the rpm and size of the scavenging gain (or "no scavenging" with a stock cam, whose overlap is negative).
LV3 · THE PHYSICS
The pulse leaves at exhaust-valve opening, reflects as a rarefaction, and is useful if it arrives during overlap. A 500 mm primary at 8000 rpm has a round trip of only about 72 crank degrees against a window of about 230, so the wave bounces several times on the way; a header is cut for the k-th round trip, usually k = 3, which gives the 450–550 mm primaries people build. The model solves for the length that tunes your engine at its target rpm and keeps it routable (350–900 mm).
Waves lose strength to the pipe walls (Kirchhoff's boundary-layer damping), α = √(ων/2) · (1 + (γ−1)/√Pr) / (r·c), and both waves swing both ways: −cos(πn) is +1 at a tuned order and −1 between them, so a mismatched pipe costs VE. The suction only helps through the small curtain both valves have open, averaged over the overlap window.

Back-pressure & residuals

LV1 · REAL LIFE
Every restriction downstream is pressure the piston has to push against.
Try blowing out through a thin straw: it takes effort. The pistons feel the same thing pushing exhaust through the manifold, catalyst and silencer. That back-pressure costs power twice: once as work on every exhaust stroke, and again because burnt gas stays behind in the cylinder, hot, taking up room the fresh charge wanted.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
Back-pressure is a tax on everything. Whatever the piston cannot push out, it pays for twice: once as pumping work on the exhaust stroke, and again as residual gas — hot burnt charge left in the clearance volume that takes the place of fresh air. The pumping loop is simply pexhaust − pintake, so a free-flowing exhaust is worth real power and a restrictive one is worth less than the back-pressure number alone suggests.
LV3 · THE PHYSICS
Back-pressure is Darcy-Weisbach friction down the primary at the velocity actually flowing, plus everything after the collector (a system sized for the engine's own flow: about 105 m/s mean gas speed for a stock system, 78 sport, 58 race), plus the catalyst as a porous plug: Δp = f · (L/D) · ½ρv² The burnt gas left behind depends on exhaust pressure against intake pressure, which matters enormously on a turbo engine: fres = 1/(rc − 1) · (pexh / pint)1/γ The VE loss is the excess over the balanced case pexh = pint. The residual is also hot, about 1000 K at full load, so it warms the fresh charge and moves it toward knock.

LV1 · REAL LIFE
Pick a part, change a dimension, and compare the new curve with the one the engine has now.
Much of an engine's breathing is bought in. Aftermarket makers sell exhausts, intake manifolds and air filters, and tuners have their own made: a header welded from bent tube, runners cut to a length, a filter element sized for the flow. Turbochargers

LV1 · REAL LIFE
One shaft: exhaust spins the turbine, the turbine spins the compressor, the compressor squeezes the air.
A turbocharger is two fans on one shaft. The hot exhaust spins the turbine on one side; the turbine drives the compressor on the other, which squeezes the intake air so each cylinder swallows more of it. The shaft can spin at 150,000–250,000 rpm. It is not free power: the turbine is a restriction in the exhaust, and the engine pays for the boost by pushing against it.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
A turbo is a restriction you chose on purpose. The turbine sits in the exhaust and is a nozzle: gas can only leave through it, so manifold pressure rises until the flow fits. That pressure is what spins the compressor — and it is also back-pressure the engine has to push against. Boost is never free; it is bought with exhaust energy and paid for in pumping work. The wastegate is what holds a target: once the turbine can make more power than the compressor needs, the gate opens and dumps the surplus around it. Without one, routing every gram of exhaust through a fixed throat gives 3.5–4.5 bar, not the 0.5–1.5 a real engine runs.
Boost is not free, and the frame has a size. A compressor makes pressure by spinning, and that shaft speed has to be bought with exhaust energy — so the more boost you ask a given turbo for, the later it arrives and the longer it takes to build. The same small frame that is fully lit by 1200 rpm at 0.5 bar does not reach half of 2.5 bar until nearly 3000 rpm. The spool figure quoted in the design summary is for the boost you actually asked for, not a fixed property of the frame.
LV3 · THE PHYSICS
The turbine is a nozzle: exhaust can only leave through its throat, so manifold pressure rises until the flow fits, and chokes at the critical ratio (2/(γ+1))γ/(γ−1) ≈ 0.54. The throat comes from the housing's A/R: A = (A/R) · Rcentroid. Its efficiency follows the blade speed ratio, tip speed over the jet speed C = √(2 cp T3 (1 − PR−k)), peaking at 0.7 (Watson & Janota): η/ηmax = 2x − x²,   x = (U/C)/0.7 The compressor follows Euler's turbomachinery relation, Δ T0 = σ U² / cp (slip σ 0.9), and absorbs Pc = ṁ cp T1 (PR0.283 − 1) / ηc Steady boost is where turbine power equals compressor power. The turbine is fed by each cylinder's blowdown pulse, not a mean pressure: a share of the gas leaves in a short burst at high pressure, and nozzle work rises with pressure ratio, so pulses spool a turbo earlier. A tight manifold keeps the pulse; a big one smooths it out. Sequential pairs stage the second turbo in at the lowest speed where the two can hold the target.
Known limit: the turbine is credited with each cylinder's blowdown pulse, timed from the cam, but a twin-scroll manifold that keeps the pulses apart is not rewarded yet. The turbine throat is taken at the volute's centroid radius, as A/R defines it (build 133; it was the wheel's radius, which made every turbine smaller than the frame it names). The compressor's efficiency island follows its speed line, so a turbo asked for low boost at low flow compresses efficiently, as on a real map.

Compressor maps & rated flow

LV1 · REAL LIFE
A compressor map: pressure ratio up, airflow across, efficiency islands in the middle.
Every compressor comes with a map, the chart turbo shops match engines with. Up the side is pressure ratio (boost), along the bottom is airflow. In the middle are the efficiency islands, where the wheel squeezes air without heating it much. On the left is the surge line, where too little flow makes the wheel stall and bark; on the right is choke, where the wheel simply cannot pass any more air. A turbo is "the right size" when the engine's operating line runs through the middle of the islands.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
Each frame also has a rated flow — small 32, medium 55, large 82 lb/min, and multi-turbo configs flow more (twin ×1.85, sequential ×1.55, compound ×2.3). That is the axis a real compressor map is drawn against. Ask an engine to move more air than its turbo is rated for and you are off the map: compressor efficiency falls away, the top end chokes off, and — the one that really bites — the charge arrives hot. Squeezing air heats it by T·(PR0.283 − 1) / efficiency, which grows faster than the boost does and grows again as efficiency drops. A 2.0 L asking 2.5 bar of a small frame wants 1.7× its rated flow, and the charge reaches the valves at 74 °C where the right frame delivers 58 °C — so it knocks, makes 120 hp less than the medium frame, and is slower round a lap than the same engine at 0.8 bar. Nothing stops you building it; it simply isn't the way to make power.
Pick the frame with TURBO SIZE (small, medium, large: 54, 71 and 86 mm compressor wheels) or enter a real COMPRESSOR WHEEL diameter. The design summary tells you how hard your engine is leaning on it.
LV3 · THE PHYSICS
Squeezing air heats it. The temperature rise across the compressor is ΔT = T1 · (PR(γ−1)/γ − 1) / ηc = T1 · (PR0.283 − 1) / ηc At 2.0 PR from 25 °C and 72% efficiency that is about 90 K. The efficiency is read off the island at the operating point: the flow through one wheel over that wheel's choke flow, and the island follows the speed line, so a turbo asked for low boost at low flow is efficient as on a real map. Near choke it falls away, the charge arrives hotter, and the engine is limited by the choke flow itself: VE is scaled down so demand never exceeds it.

Wastegate & boost control

LV1 · REAL LIFE
The gate stays shut while boost builds, then opens and lets the surplus exhaust bypass the turbine.
Left alone, a turbo would keep making more boost the harder the engine runs, until something broke. The wastegate is a valve that lets exhaust bypass the turbine. It stays shut while boost builds, then opens just enough to hold the target. The boost you set is really a wastegate setting.
Rally cars add anti-lag: fuel and air burned in the hot exhaust keep the turbine spinning even off the throttle, with the famous bangs, at the cost of a very hot turbo.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
BOOST TARGET sets the gate. BOOST TAPER FROM and BOOST AT LIMITER make the ECU wind the target down toward the redline, as many factory tunes do to protect the engine where the rods are most loaded. ECU TORQUE LIMIT (Nm, 0 = off) holds the engine to a calibrated torque by asking for less boost wherever it would make more: the flat "350 Nm from 1500 to 4400 rpm" plateaus makers quote are this, not the engine's own curve. ANTI-LAG keeps the turbo spooled (and wears the engine and turbo, see Wear & reliability).
LV3 · THE PHYSICS
The steady state is a power balance with two paths for the exhaust: find p3 such that Pturbine(p3) = Pcompressor(target) Whatever flow the turbine does not swallow at that pressure goes through the gate. If the turbine cannot make the power even swallowing everything, the gate stays shut and the engine is below its target: that is the region below the boost threshold. Without a gate, routing all the exhaust through a fixed throat gives 3.5–4.5 bar of boost on typical engines, which is why holding a target is the gate's whole job. A taper scales the target linearly from its start rpm to the chosen share at the limiter. A torque limit is solved once per engine: every 200 rpm the boost at which the engine makes exactly the limit is found by bisection on its own torque, and the gate (or the vanes, or a supercharger's bypass) is asked for the lower of that and the tapered target. Like the taper it is calibration, not physics: the engine under it is unchanged and only meets a lower target. Boosted engines only.

Turbo lag

LV1 · REAL LIFE
Floor it and the throttle is open at once. The boost has to wait for the shaft.
Floor a turbo car from low revs and for a moment not much happens, then the boost arrives with a shove. That delay is lag: the turbo's shaft has to be spun up by the exhaust before it can make pressure, and at low revs there is not much exhaust to do it. Small turbos spool quickly but run out of breath; big ones make huge power but wake up late.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
Lag is real in the 0–100 now. The acceleration run spins the turbo shaft alongside the car and reads torque off a two-dimensional rpm × boost map, because boost is a state with a history: at 4000 rpm a turbo might be at 0.1 bar because you have just floored it, or at full boost because you have been flat for two seconds. A single curve can only describe the second. The car also leaves the line with the turbo already part-lit, which is what brake-boosting is.
The lap solver runs the same shaft along the lap (build 118): boost builds while you are flat, bleeds away when you lift, brake and change gear, and the drive at each point is scaled by the boost actually there. So a laggy build loses time on a lap as well as off the line.
LV3 · THE PHYSICS
Spool is the shaft's equation of motion, integrated as energy so it can start from rest: E = ½ J ω²    dE/dt = Pturbine − Pcompressor − Pbearings − Pfill J scales with the wheel size. The bearings follow Petroff's law for the floating-ring journals, P = 2π μ r³ L ω² / c (about 0.8 kW at 140,000 rpm on a typical cartridge, within published 0.2–0.8 kW). Raising boost also means filling the pipes and intercooler, dm = V dp / (R T), each kilogram costing compressor work. Because boost has a history, the 0–100 run reads torque off a two-dimensional rpm × boost map instead of one curve.

Variable geometry turbos

LV1 · REAL LIFE
Pivoting vanes close the turbine's throat for early boost and open it for top-end flow.
A turbine housing has one throat size, and it is a compromise: small enough to spool early chokes the engine up top. A variable geometry turbo fits a ring of pivoting vanes inside the housing, like window blinds. Closed, they speed up the gas onto the wheel for early boost; open, they get out of the way. Almost every modern diesel has one. Petrol engines almost never do, because their exhaust is too hot for the vanes (Porsche's 911 Turbo is the famous exception, with exotic alloys).
LV2 · IN THE GAME
TURBINE A/R is the housing itself, and it is your call. TIGHT spools earlier and costs top end; LOOSE breathes at the top and spools later; STOCK is the frame's usual housing. Which side to take is a design decision, not physics — VW fits the GTI a small, tight turbine for response and accepts the power it costs. The model will not choose for you.

VARIABLE GEOMETRY is the throat as a control, and it is why diesels spool. A fixed housing has one throat area, and that one number is a compromise: tight enough to light at 1700 rpm is far too tight to breathe at 4500. A VGT puts a ring of pivoting vanes in the housing, so the ECU picks the throat at every operating point — shut for a quick spool, open to get out of the way once the target is made. Set TURBINE to VARIABLE GEOMETRY and watch the torque plateau walk down the rev range: a 2.0 diesel goes from full boost at 2575 rpm to full boost at 1800, and its torque peak moves from 2700 rpm to 1900. That is not a bonus applied to diesels, it is geometry — the ring's sweep (about 2.9:1, from 21° to 60° of vane angle) and its top-end loss (the vanes occupy 8–13% of the circumference, so a VGT at full open flows less than the plain housing of the same frame) both come straight out of vane count, thickness and the two end stops.
So why does nothing petrol-powered have one? Two reasons, both of which you can see in the app. The vane ring is the hottest moving part in the exhaust, and nickel alloys creep above about 1100 K. A diesel is quality-governed — always lean — so its gas arrives at the vanes near 880 K; a petrol engine runs rich for power and sends 1235 K. The design panel prints that temperature and tells you when you are over the limit, and the reliability score falls about 19 points when you are. The other reason is simpler: a vane ring, its bearings and an actuator cost about 1.7× a plain housing. Fit one to a petrol engine and it works beautifully and does not last — which is exactly its reputation.
LV3 · THE PHYSICS
The throat of a vane ring is the gap between neighbouring vanes. With Z vanes of thickness t on a pitch circle of radius r2, passage height h and vane angle β from tangential: A(β) = h · (2π r2 sin β − Z · t) A sweep from 21° to 60° gives about 2.9:1 of flow range, and at full open the vanes still block 8–13% of the circumference, so a VGT flows less than a plain housing of the same frame. The ECU schedules the widest throat that still holds the target, since every extra square millimetre closed is back-pressure. Nickel-alloy vanes creep above about 1100 K; a diesel's gas arrives near 880 K and a petrol engine's near 1235 K.

Superchargers

LV1 · REAL LIFE
A Roots blower: two lobed rotors, belt-driven from the crank, carry air around the outside.
A supercharger is a compressor driven by a belt from the crankshaft instead of by the exhaust. Because it turns with the engine, boost is there the instant you open the throttle: no lag. The price is the power it takes to drive it. Roots blowers (the classic drag-racing blower sticking out of the bonnet) and twin-screw blowers move a fixed volume every turn and give nearly full boost from idle; a centrifugal supercharger is a turbo-style compressor on a belt, and its boost rises with rpm.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
Choose INTAKE TYPE supercharger and a SUPERCHARGER type. The whine you hear is the blower's lobes or blades passing, see Options, sound & tips.
LV3 · THE PHYSICS
Boost against rpm follows each type's character: boost fraction = lo + (hi − lo) · (N / Nredline)e Roots 0.82 → 1 (linear), twin-screw 0.78 → 1, centrifugal 0.04 → 1 with e = 2. The drive's cost is charged as friction, 0.18 · drag · (MAP − 1) · (0.4 + 0.6 N/Nredline) bar of FMEP, with the Roots the hungriest (1.25) and the centrifugal the least (0.80). The compressed charge is heated by the same T(PR0.283 − 1)/η as a turbo's.
Limit: a supercharger's boost curve and drive loss are hand-set laws per type, not derived from rotor geometry.

Intercoolers

LV1 · REAL LIFE
Hot compressed air in, cool dense air out.
Compressing air heats it: a turbo at 1 bar of boost can send air out at 100 °C or more. Hot air is thin (less oxygen per litre) and it makes the engine knock. An intercooler is a radiator for the intake air, sitting in the airflow at the front of the car, that hands most of that heat to the outside air before the charge reaches the engine.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
INTERCOOLER: none, a small core or a large one. A bigger core cools better but holds more air, so the turbo takes a little longer to fill it (more lag).
LV3 · THE PHYSICS
A heat exchanger is rated by its effectiveness ε, the share of the temperature rise it gives back: Tcharge = Tamb + ΔTcomp · (1 − ε) ε is not a constant. Each kilogram of charge that passes slowly meets the same metal for longer, so a core cools better at low flow. The model uses the number of transfer units (build 136): ε = 1 − e−NTU    NTU = UA / (ṁ cp) with the cooling air's capacity large against the charge's. UA is the two sides in series: the charge side's film coefficient follows the flow as ṁ0.8 (turbulent flow in the tubes, Dittus–Boelter), the outside does not depend on it, and the two are balanced at the reference point (Shah & Sekulic). The quoted figures, 0.62 for the small core and 0.82 for the large, hold at the engine's flow at its redline at 1 bar of boost; at half that flow they rise to about 0.76 and 0.92. At the reference, the 90 K rise of 2.0 PR leaves about 34 K through the small core and 16 K through the large. The charge volume (the core holds 1.2 L small or 4.5 L large, plus 1.5 or 3 m of ducting) is what the turbo has to fill when boost rises (see Turbo lag).

Nitrous oxide

LV1 · REAL LIFE
Nitrous: oxygen in a bottle, and a lot of cold.
Nitrous oxide (N₂O) carries oxygen: about 36% by mass, against air's 23%. Sprayed into the intake with extra fuel, it breaks apart in the heat of combustion and lets the engine burn more fuel than its air alone could. It also arrives very cold, which makes the charge denser. It is power in a bottle: brutal while it lasts, and hard on pistons.

Compression & knock

LV1 · REAL LIFE
The flame sweeps from the plug; the last gas in the corner ignites on its own before it gets there. Bang.
Normally the flame sweeps smoothly across the chamber. But the gas it has not reached yet, the end gas, is being squeezed and heated by the burning charge behind it. If it gets hot enough for long enough, it ignites on its own, all at once: knock (detonation, pinking). The pressure spike rings the chamber like a bell and you hear a metallic rattle. Sustained, it melts pistons and breaks ring lands.
Everything that makes the end gas hotter or gives it more time makes knock worse: high compression, boost, hot intake air, low octane, advanced timing, low revs at full load.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
Why boosted engines run low compression and lose timing. Higher manifold pressure means more residual trapped, and residual is hot — it drags the end gas toward detonation before the flame gets there. That is why a turbo engine is built at 8.5–9.5:1 where an NA engine of the same era runs 11:1 or more, and why the ECU is pulling timing long before you see the knock bar move. Push boost up on a high-compression engine and you can watch it happen: the AUTO ignition retards, and the power stops following the boost.
The KNOCK bar shows how close the engine is. On AUTO timing the ECU pulls spark to stay just short of it; on FIXED you can drive straight into it. Above about 60 on the knock scale the engine loses power fast (up to a third), and with WEAR on detonation is the quickest way to blow it up.
LV3 · THE PHYSICS
Knock is the end gas's own chemistry. It autoignites when the Livengood–Wu integral of its ignition delay reaches one, ∫ dt / τ(p, T) = 1,    τ[ms] = 17.68 · (ON/100)3.402 · p[atm]−1.7 · e3800/T the delay being Douaud & Eyzat's correlation for a fuel of octane number ON. The gas is traced on this model's own cycle: it starts as fresh charge at the intake temperature mixed with the hot residual, T = (1 + f)/(1/Tfresh + f/Tres), is compressed by the piston, then by the Wiebe burn phased at the spark's CA50, following the pressure isentropically, until 90% has burned. A real fuel autoignites like the reference fuel of its octane index, OI = RON − K·S (Kalghatgi), where S = RON − MON and K depends on how hot the gas is at 15 bar: boosted engines compress colder gas and have negative K, so a sensitive fuel such as E85 resists better than its RON says.
The knock score is 40 × the integral (40 = onset). A knocking chamber rings at its first acoustic mode, f = 1.84 c / (π B), about 6 kHz in an 84 mm bore, and that is the ring you hear in Options, sound & tips.
Limit: rotaries and two-strokes still use the older points score for knock, because their end gas is not a four-stroke's.

Fuels & octane

LV1 · REAL LIFE
Higher octane means the fuel resists knock longer. It does not contain more energy.
Octane is a fuel's resistance to knock, measured in a special test engine against a mix of two reference fuels: iso-octane (100) and heptane (0). There are two standard tests: RON (gentle, the number on European pumps) and MON (hot and harsh); the gap between them is the fuel's sensitivity.
Premium fuel has no more energy than regular. It just lets the engine use more compression, boost and timing without knocking. Alcohols like E85 and methanol have very high octane and cool the charge as they evaporate, which is why tuners love them, but they carry much less energy per litre, so the engine needs far more of them.

Wear & reliability

LV1 · REAL LIFE
Push too hard for too long and something lets go.
Engines rarely wear out gently in racing; they get killed. The usual killers are detonation (knock hammers the pistons), overheating, lean mixtures under boost (burnt pistons and valves), over-revving (bent valves, broken rods), cold oil worked hard, and nitrous or anti-lag used too freely. A well-tuned engine that avoids all of them can run for hundreds of thousands of kilometres.

Emissions & the catalyst

LV1 · REAL LIFE
A three-way catalyst turns CO, HC and NOx into CO₂, water and nitrogen, but only near λ 1.
An engine's exhaust carries three regulated pollutants. CO (carbon monoxide) comes from fuel that ran out of oxygen: rich mixtures. HC (unburned hydrocarbons) is fuel that did not burn at all. NOx (nitrogen oxides) forms when the flame is very hot with oxygen to spare: slightly lean, high load.
A three-way catalytic converter cleans all three at once, but only in a narrow window around the chemically exact mixture, λ = 1. That is why modern petrol engines hold stoich almost everywhere. Diesels run lean, so they need a particle filter (DPF) for soot and urea injection (SCR) for NOx.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
The EMISSIONS readout comes from a simulated exhaust analyser. Choose the CATALYTIC CONV.: stock (97% conversion), sport (85%) or none. The scorecard's emit score rates a stoich cruise and part load.
LV3 · THE PHYSICS
Engine-out emissions are correlations with mixture and combustion temperature: CO high rich, near zero at and lean of stoich; HC U-shaped (rich and misfire-lean), plus valve overlap, carburettor metering and a cold engine; NOx peaking near λ 1.08 and growing with load, compression, spark advance and charge temperature. The catalyst's oxidation of CO and HC needs spare oxygen (works stoich to lean) and its reduction of NOx needs none (stoich to rich), so its conversion falls off on both sides of λ = 1.
Limit: emissions are correlations with the right trends, not combustion chemistry (no Zeldovich NOx mechanism).

The electric drive

LV1 · REAL LIFE
Battery, inverter, motor: the whole drivetrain of an electric car.
An electric car has three main parts. The battery stores energy as direct current. The inverter switches that into three-phase alternating current at whatever frequency the motor needs. The motor turns the current into torque. One fixed reduction gear takes the torque to the wheels: there is no clutch, no gearbox and no idle.
The motor gives its full torque from standstill and holds it up to a speed called the knee. Above the knee the power stays roughly flat and the torque falls. Braking runs the motor backwards as a generator and puts energy back in the battery: regenerative braking.
A battery's voltage falls as it empties. The car's computer draws more current to make up for it, until the battery runs low. Then it cuts the power to protect the cells, and when the battery is nearly empty it allows only a little: limp mode.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
Building one. On the ENGINE tab set ENGINE TYPE to ELECTRIC. The combustion sections give way to Motor, More motors, Inverter and Battery, each explained on its own page. Press BUILD & DYNO as for an engine. The presets include three Teslas: the Model 3 RWD, the Model Y Long Range and the Model S Plaid.
On the LIVE bench. THROTTLE asks for torque: a fixed share of what the drive gives at that speed when it is at its best (a full battery, a cool motor, no damage). The controller draws whatever current that takes, so a part throttle holds its power as the battery drains. LOAD is the dyno brake. The tiles show POWER, TORQUE, BATTERY (kW drawn), DRIVE EFF, CHARGE, WINDING temperature, MOTOR HEALTH, PACK CURRENT (A) and POWER LIMIT. POWER LIMIT says what is holding the power below the request, and how much of the request you are getting:
• NONE: you get all you asked for.
• BATTERY: the battery's voltage or current limit.
• THERMAL: the winding or the magnets are too hot.

In a race the battery stands in for the fuel tank: there is no charging in the pit lane, an energy manager chooses the power that lasts the distance, and a car that empties its battery retires OUT OF CHARGE.
LV3 · THE PHYSICS
The battery gives the shaft's power plus every loss on the way: the copper's I²R, the iron's hysteresis and eddy currents, friction and windage, and the inverter's conduction and switching. Ppack = T·ω + Pcu + Pfe + Pmech + Pinv The battery is an open-circuit voltage that follows its charge, behind an internal resistance: V = Voc(SoC) − I·R The current solves P = V·I. A pack can give no more than its rated current, and never so much that its voltage falls below the cells' cut-off.
Limits, in the order they bite.
• Battery temperature: the battery management cuts the power from 50 °C, to nothing at 60 °C.
• Winding temperature: the controller cuts the current from 150 °C, to nothing at 180 °C.
• Magnet temperature: it does the same over the last 20 °C below the magnet grade's limit.
• Low charge: below 15% the battery management allows a share of what the cells could give, falling straight to 30% at 2%.
• Limp mode: below 2% it holds the power to a tenth of the battery's rated power (its power at 50% charge).
Damage the controller cannot prevent is permanent: magnets heated past their grade's limit lose flux, and a winding above 200 °C cooks its insulation.
The 0-100 and the lap run at 50% charge. A race starts at 95% and stops at 5%.

The motor

LV1 · REAL LIFE
A magnet rotor turning inside a stator of copper windings.
A traction motor has two parts. The stator is a stack of thin steel sheets with copper windings in slots, and it stays still. The rotor spins inside it. Current in the windings makes a magnetic field that rotates, and the rotor follows it.
In a permanent-magnet motor the rotor carries magnets and turns exactly with the field. In an induction motor the rotor is a cage of copper bars: the stator's field induces currents in the cage, which slip a little behind the field and are pulled along.
Torque grows with the rotor's volume and with the current. Spinning makes the motor a generator too: its back-EMF rises with speed. Where it reaches the battery's voltage (the knee), the controller weakens the field to keep going. Above the knee the power stays about flat and the torque falls.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
MOTOR TYPE.
• PERMANENT MAGNET: the most torque per amp and the best efficiency. Its magnets cost money, lose strength when hot, and drag a little (iron loss) even when it gives no torque.
• INDUCTION (COPPER CAGE): no magnets. It buys its field with current, so it makes less torque per amp, and its rotor heats up. With no torque asked it has no field and no drag, which is why Tesla puts one on the front axle of the Model 3 and Model Y.
ROTOR DIAMETER (80–260 mm). Torque grows with the diameter squared. A bigger rotor is heavier, has more inertia and reaches its surface-speed limit at lower rpm.
STACK LENGTH (40–250 mm). Torque grows in proportion to the length, with no effect on the speed limits.
POLES (4–12). More poles mean shorter end windings and a thinner, lighter core. They also raise the electrical frequency for a given speed: more iron loss, and a lower top speed from the inverter's frequency limit.
TURNS PER PHASE (4–120). More turns give more torque per amp but more back-EMF, so the knee comes at lower rpm and there is less power at speed. Fewer turns move the knee up and need more current for the same torque. Match the turns to the battery's voltage.
MAGNETS.
• FERRITE: cheap and survives 250 °C, but it is a third as strong, so it needs twice the volume.
• NdFeB (SH): neodymium, the standard; strong, with a limit of 150 °C.
• NdFeB + Dy (UH): dysprosium raises the limit to 180 °C for slightly less strength and a higher price.
MAGNET RETENTION. What holds the magnets in at speed. IRON BRIDGES in the steel sheets allow a rotor surface speed of 150 m/s. A CARBON SLEEVE wound over the rotor allows 220 m/s, which is how the Plaid's large rotors reach 20,000 rpm.
MOTOR COOLING. Decides how long the peak lasts and what the motor holds for good.
• AIR (FINNED): free, but weak.
• WATER JACKET: coolant round the stator.
• OIL SPRAY: the jacket, plus oil sprayed on the end windings and the rotor.
• DIRECT SLOT: coolant inside the slots, against the copper; the best and the dearest.
MAX SPEED (rpm). The design speed. The motor's real top speed is the lowest of this, the rotor's surface-speed limit and the inverter's frequency limit. Watch the summary under the form.
LV3 · THE PHYSICS
Torque is the flux linkage times the current: T = 3/2 · p · ψ · I, ψ = kw · N · (2/π) · Bg · τp · L Here p is the pole pairs, N the turns per phase, kw = 0.93 the winding factor, τp the pole pitch (πD/poles) and L the stack. The air-gap flux Bg is 0.85 times the magnets' remanence for a magnet motor (1.28 T for SH NdFeB, 0.42 T for ferrite). An induction motor runs 0.8 T and pays for it with a magnetising current, which takes its share of the inverter's amps.
The knee is where the back-EMF ω·ψ, with the winding's voltage drop on top (×1.29 at full current), meets the battery's voltage. Above it the power holds to about 4× the knee speed for a magnet motor and 3× for an induction motor.
Losses. Copper resistance comes from the slot area (40% copper fill) and rises 0.393% per kelvin. Iron loss is Steinmetz's law on 0.35 mm silicon steel.
Top speed. The lowest of three: MAX SPEED; the rotor surface speed v/(πD); and the inverter's output frequency, 1.2 kHz for IGBTs and 2 kHz for SiC, divided by the pole pairs.
Heat. Three lumped nodes (winding, stator core, rotor) and the coolant.
Calibration. Three constants come from one measured motor, the Tesla Model 3 rear (MotorXP, 2020). The 2012 Nissan Leaf as ORNL measured it is the check.

More motors

LV1 · REAL LIFE
A motor on each axle makes an electric car four-wheel drive with no driveshaft.
An electric car becomes four-wheel drive by adding a second motor on the other axle; no driveshaft or centre differential is needed. Tesla's dual-motor Model 3 and Model Y pair a magnet motor at the rear with an induction motor at the front, which can idle along at a cruise without dragging. The Model S Plaid has three motors, two at the rear, one for each wheel, and one at the front.
A hybrid can do the same: the Porsche 918 Spyder drives its front wheels with an electric motor of their own while the engine drives the rear.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
REAR MOTORS (electric cars only). 1, or 2 (ONE PER WHEEL): two of the main motor, twice the torque, the mass and the cost.
FRONT MOTOR. NONE, PERMANENT MAGNET or INDUCTION (COPPER CAGE). Fitting one makes the car all-wheel drive and locks the drivetrain field on the VEHICLE tab; taking it off gives the old drive back.
The front motor has its own FRONT ROTOR DIAMETER, FRONT STACK LENGTH, FRONT POLES, FRONT TURNS PER PHASE and FRONT CURRENT, which work as on The motor. It shares the main motor's magnets, retention, cooling, inverter type and max speed.
FRONT REDUCTION (hybrids only). The gear ratio from the front motor to the wheels. Leave it at 0 to have it matched, so the motor's top speed falls at the car's top speed. Above its top speed the front motor is disconnected.
LV3 · THE PHYSICS
On an electric car every motor drives through the same reduction, so they turn at one speed and act as one motor. Their torques, masses, costs and inertias add; the top speed is the lowest of theirs. The controller splits the torque between the axles to lose the least: at a cruise an induction front motor carries nothing, and flat out both give all they have. They share one cooling loop, and the controller's limits are taken at the lowest of their rotors'.
On a hybrid the front motor has its own reduction. Its force at the wheels is F = T · ifront · 0.97 / rtyre and it is added to the gearbox's in the 0-100, the top speed and the lap. The two motors share the battery's power in proportion to their peak powers, and the lap's charge balance counts what the front one draws. It does not regenerate; only the main motor does.

The inverter

LV1 · REAL LIFE
Fast switches chop the battery's steady voltage into the motor's three-phase current.
The battery gives a steady voltage; the motor needs three alternating currents that follow the rotor. The inverter makes them with six power switches, turning them on and off thousands of times a second so that the average current traces a sine wave.
Older and cheaper inverters use silicon IGBTs. Newer ones use silicon carbide (SiC) MOSFETs, which switch faster and lose less, especially at part load. Tesla moved the Model 3 to SiC in 2017. The inverter's current rating sets the motor's torque, since torque is current times flux.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
SWITCHES.
• SILICON IGBT: cheaper. It has a fixed voltage drop that costs most at part load, and it limits the output frequency to 1.2 kHz.
• SiC MOSFET: no fixed drop, a fifth of the switching loss, 2 kHz. It costs nearly twice as much per amp.
CURRENT RATING (100–1600 A rms). The most current the inverter gives the motor. Torque rises with it; so do the copper's heat (with the square of the current) and the price. The battery has to be able to supply it too: watch the summary for which one limits.
LV3 · THE PHYSICS
Losses in the three phase legs: Pinv = 3·(0.9·V0·I + Ron·I²) + 3·(√2/π)·tsw·fsw·Vdc·I IGBT: V0 = 0.8 V knee, switching at 10 kHz, tsw = 0.23 µs. That is an 820 A automotive module losing about 75 mJ per on-off cycle at 400 V.
SiC: no knee, 16 kHz, tsw = 0.05 µs.
On-resistance is for a 1000 A device and scales inversely with the rating. The output frequency limit caps the motor's speed at f·60/p rpm. Cost is $6 per amp of rating for IGBT and $11 for SiC.

The battery

LV1 · REAL LIFE
Cells in series set the voltage; the capacity sets how far it goes.
A traction battery is hundreds or thousands of cells. Cells in series add their voltages; groups in parallel add their capacity and the current they can give. The chemistry decides how much energy a kilogram holds, how hard it can be discharged and charged, how long it lasts and what it costs.
A cell's voltage falls as it empties, and its internal resistance turns some of the current into heat. A battery that is cold, hot or nearly empty gives less power.
Prices have fallen tenfold: about $1100 per kWh in 2010, $160 in 2019 and $115 in 2024.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
CHEMISTRY. Each is offered only from the year it reached cars.
• LEAD-ACID: heavy (about 40 Wh/kg) and slow to charge; the 1990s.
• NiMH: from 1996; tough, about 70 Wh/kg.
• LFP (lithium iron phosphate): from 2005; cheap, safe and long-lived, about 175 Wh/kg by 2023; a flat voltage curve and a low cell voltage (3.2 V). From 2020 it is packed cell-to-pack, which wastes the least weight.
• NCA / NMC: from 2008; the energy chemistry of long-range cars, about 270 Wh/kg, 3.7 V cells.
• NCA (POWER CELLS): from 2012; the same chemistry built for current, as in the Plaid. Less energy per kilogram, but nearly twice the discharge rate.
• Li-ION (HYBRID CELLS) and NiMH (HYBRID CELLS): built for hybrids, a small battery that gives and takes 20–25 times its capacity in power.
CELLS IN SERIES (20–400). Sets the voltage: 96 NCA cells make about 350 V, 110 make about 400 V. Higher voltage moves the motor's knee up (more power at speed) and lowers the current for the same power.
CAPACITY (kWh). Range, mass and price. A bigger battery also gives and takes more power, because there are more cells in parallel.
TECHNOLOGY YEAR. Each chemistry's energy per kilogram and price per kWh follow the year: a 2010 battery is heavier and dearer than the same battery in 2024.
LV3 · THE PHYSICS
Each chemistry is a table of open-circuit voltage against charge, a cut-off voltage, a resistance-capacity product (Ω·Ah), a sustained discharge rate (C) and a charge rate it accepts under braking.
The pack. Nominal voltage is the cells in series times the mean cell voltage. The capacity in Ah is kWh·1000 / Vnom. The resistance is R = Ns · (R·Ah)cell / Ah · 1.1 (+10% for busbars, contactors and the fuse).
Limits. The most current is the C-rate times the Ah (NCA 6C, power cells 10C, hybrid cells 25C). The most power is reached either at that current or at the cut-off voltage, whichever comes first. The charge rate under braking is the lithium-plating limit: about 1C for NCA, 1.5C for LFP.
Mass. The cells' mass is kWh over Wh/kg at the year, divided by the cells' share of the pack (0.62–0.68, or 0.82 cell-to-pack).
Heat. The pack is one lumped node: I²R heat in, a liquid cooling plate taking about 2 W/K per kWh to 30 °C coolant. Its management cuts power from 50 °C.

Hybrids

LV1 · REAL LIFE
Engine, motor and battery: power flows between all three.
A hybrid adds an electric motor and a small battery to an engine. Braking charges the battery. The motor helps the engine when it is least efficient (pulling away, low in the revs) and drives the car alone at low speed with the engine stopped. There are three main layouts:
• Parallel (P2): the motor sits between the engine's clutch and the gearbox and turns with the engine; both drive the wheels through the gears. Hyundai, BMW and Porsche use it.
• Power-split: a planetary gear set divides the engine's power between the wheels and a generator, so the engine runs at any speed it likes: an electric CVT. This is Toyota's system.
• Series: the engine only drives a generator; a motor alone drives the wheels (Nissan e-POWER).
A plug-in hybrid has a bigger battery charged from the mains, and drives tens of kilometres on it before it starts behaving like an ordinary hybrid.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
HYBRID on the ENGINE tab adds the system to any combustion engine:
• PARALLEL (P2): the motor's torque adds to the engine's at engine speed, through the gearbox.
• POWER-SPLIT (eCVT): Toyota's planetary; the car has no gears, only one reduction.
• SERIES: a generator sized from the engine, and the traction motor alone at the wheels.
The Motor, Inverter and Battery sections then design the hybrid's motor and battery, and the hybrid tune appears.
ASSIST (P2 only). When the motor helps at full throttle:
• FULL: everything it has.
• ECO (TORQUE FILL): only to fill the engine's curve up to its peak torque.
• ABOVE RPM or BELOW RPM: only past, or short of, the ASSIST RPM.
PLUG-IN. YES starts the battery full and drives it down first. The VEHICLE tab then gives the WLTP figures a plug-in is homologated with: weighted consumption, electric range and kWh/100 km. In a race it starts full and spreads that charge over the distance.

• AUTO: the controller chooses.
• EV: electric only, until the battery runs low.
• HOLD: keeps the charge where it is.
• CHARGE: the engine charges the battery.
• SPORT: the motor always helps.
• ENGINE ONLY.
• MANUAL: you set the motor command and, on a power-split, the engine's speed.
VIEW shows the power flow, the power-split's lever diagram, or the engine's BSFC map with its operating point. The pedal asks for the engine's torque plus the motor's, so from about half throttle the engine runs at full load: give it the cooling for that.
Presets: the Hyundai Ioniq Hybrid, the Toyota Prius, the Nissan Qashqai e-POWER, the BMW 330e and the Porsche 918 Spyder.
LV3 · THE PHYSICS
The power-split. The planetary obeys Willis's equation, with Toyota's 30/78-tooth gear set and MG2 at 65:17 to the ring, on a 600 V bus: nsun · Zs + nring · Zr = ncarrier · (Zs + Zr) MG1 holds the engine on its optimal operating line, the lowest fuel for each power.
The series hybrid. The generator is sized for 110% of the engine's peak torque and 105% of its power.
Laps and races. The lap balances the battery over each lap: it is solved at full assist and solved again with the assist cut until what the motor spends equals what braking returns.
The WLTP cycle. An equivalent-consumption energy manager (ECMS) prices the battery's energy in fuel and chooses each second between the engine off, load-point shifting and assist. If the charge does not balance, the cycle is corrected as WLTP corrects a hybrid's.
Plug-ins. Run the regulation's procedure (EU 2017/1151): the cycle from a full battery until it sustains its charge, weighted by the utility factor.

The hybrid tune

LV1 · REAL LIFE
The charge window, and where in it the controller aims.
A hybrid's behaviour is a calibration: rules its engineers write into the controller. When does the motor help, and how much? When does the car drive on electricity alone, and how fast? How hard does braking charge the battery? Between what charge levels may the battery swing, and where should it sit?
A frugal road car keeps the engine off as much as it can. A racing hybrid spends every joule it can get, and racing classes limit how much energy the battery may give and take back on each lap.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
HYBRID TUNE on the ENGINE tab is saved with the build. The 0-100, the lap, the race, the WLTP cycle and the LIVE bench all read it.
ASSIST RPM. The engine speed that ASSIST's ABOVE RPM and BELOW RPM settings use.
REGENERATION. HIGH, LOW (half) or OFF: how hard braking charges the battery.
LOAD-POINT SHIFTING. ON lets the engine work harder than the car needs and store the surplus, which moves it to where it burns least. OFF charges only from braking and from a low battery.
EV UP TO (km/h, 0 = ANY). The fastest the car drives with the engine off.
EV POWER (kW, 0 = AUTO). The most power it asks of the battery before starting the engine.
CHARGE LOW / TARGET / HIGH (%). The window the battery is used in, and where the controller aims. A plug-in starts at HIGH and drives down to TARGET.
DEPLOY LIMIT (MJ per lap, 0 = none). The most energy the motors may take from the battery on a lap.
HARVEST LIMIT (MJ per lap, 0 = none). The most braking may put back on a lap; past it the friction brakes take the rest.
ASSIST MAP. The share of the motor's help over shaft speed (columns) and pedal (rows). Click a cell to step it down 25%; from 0 it goes back to 100%. Flat out reads the top row.
LV3 · THE PHYSICS
The motor's curve is its full torque times the assist map's share at that speed (and, on a P2, times the ASSIST window). Its regeneration is its braking torque, limited by the battery's charge rate, times REGENERATION's share (1, 0.5 or 0).
The lap's charge balance. What the motors may deploy is Edeploy ≤ min(Eharvest + Eplug-in, DEPLOY LIMIT) where Eharvest is what braking returns, to the HARVEST LIMIT, and Eplug-in is a plug-in's own charge allowed on that lap in a race. If the motors would spend more, the assist is cut in proportion and the lap is solved again.
Plug-ins in a race. The race keeps a second pace grid of laps with the motors free of the balance. A plug-in runs at a share x of those laps, chosen so that its charge between HIGH and TARGET lasts the race (with 3% in hand), and chosen again every time it crosses the line.
The WLTP cycle. The cycle's energy manager keeps the charge within the window and aims at the target.

Wheelbase, track width, centre-of-gravity height and weight: the numbers a chassis is made of.
A car's chassis decides a lot before the engine is even fitted. Its weight is what every newton of drive has to accelerate. Its wheelbase and the height of its centre of gravity decide how much weight shifts to the rear wheels when you accelerate and to the fronts when you brake. Tyres & grip

LV1 · REAL LIFE
The contact patch is about the size of your hand. The friction circle shows how its grip is shared between turning and driving.
Everything a car does goes through four patches of rubber, each about the size of a hand. A tyre grips by keying into the road's texture and by its own rubber's sticky, energy-soaking stretch. Two surprises:
· A tyre's grip does not rise in step with the weight on it. Load it twice as hard and it grips only about 1.8 times as hard. That is why weight is bad and why wide tyres help.
· Grip is shared. A tyre at its limit in a corner has nothing left for braking or accelerating: the friction circle. Race drivers trail the brakes into a corner to use every bit of it.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
From that baseline you tune the drivetrain (FWD/RWD/AWD — AWD launches hardest), the gearbox (type, speeds and the gearing slider), tyres (compound + width/profile/ diameter set grip and rolling radius), aero (front/rear/floor add downforce and drag), brakes (type + rotor size set stopping power, how hot the rotors run and when the pads fade) and suspension (how far the body rolls and the camber it runs). Grip is not a multiplier on any of these: a tyre grips harder the less load it carries for its size, so a bigger tyre, a lighter car and less weight thrown onto the outside tyres in a corner (a low, wide car) all corner faster, and roll that tips the loaded tyre off its best camber angle costs grip.

LV3 · THE PHYSICS
Load sensitivity. Friction falls as the tyre is loaded harder: μ = μref · (Fz / Fz,ref)−0.15 about 10% less each time the load doubles, with the reference at 60% of the tyre's rated load. The rated load follows the tyre's air volume, (π/4)(D² − d²)·w at about 16,300 kg per m³, which matches real load indices within 3%. So a bigger tyre, a lighter car and less load transfer all grip harder, by one mechanism.
Load transfer in a corner moves m · ay · h / t from the inside tyres to the outside ones (h CG height, t track width), and the outside tyres, loaded harder, use their load less well.
The friction ellipse. A tyre pulls about 15% harder along its length than across it (Milliken), so braking and traction get 1.15 μ and combined use follows an ellipse.
Cornering drag. Side force comes from running at a slip angle, which leans the force back against travel: summed over four tyres it costs (m ay)² / (C · N), C ≈ 0.2 per degree (Gillespie).
Rolling resistance rises with speed: crr(v) = crr,80 · (1 + β (v² − v80²)), from the compound's value at 80 km/h to about 1.6 times it at 200.
In a race the tyres' temperatures come out where real ones are measured: a road sport tyre's hottest corner 80–110 °C on track (pit-lane pyrometer guidance for UHP summer tyres is 66–88 °C, and a reading in the pits runs 10–20 °C under the tyre on track), a semi-slick's 85–110 °C (Michelin rates its Cup 2 R at 70–100 °C, 90 ideal), a front-driver's rears cold, the outside tyres hottest, working temperature in two or three laps, and a heavy car overheating its road tyres. Two things are unverified: an eco tyre on a light car runs cool (45–60 °C), and a fast circuit's long straights cool the tyres a little below a short circuit's.

Gearboxes

LV1 · REAL LIFE
A small gear driving a big one: slower, and stronger by the ratio of their teeth.
An engine only makes useful power over a narrow band of revs, but a car has to go from walking pace to 250 km/h. Gears solve it: a small gear driving a big one turns it slower and stronger, by the ratio of their teeth. A gearbox is a set of those ratios and a way to change between them. How quickly and smoothly it changes, and how much power it loses doing it, is what separates the types.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
Gearbox type changes three things: how long the drive is cut on an upshift, how much of the engine's output survives the driveline, and how the car leaves the line. How the ratios are spaced is a choice of its own, GEAR SPACING (see Gearing & shifting).
· Manual (H-pattern) — the most efficient box, but you lose ~350 ms per shift lifting off, moving the lever and re-engaging. Cheap. Best where there are few shifts to make.
· Sequential — a race box that bangs the next dog in: ~60 ms shifts; it is usually built with close ratios that keep a peaky engine in its band. Expensive.
· Dual-clutch (DCT) — the next gear is already engaged on the second clutch, so shifts are seamless: the drive is never cut (the change takes ~30 ms). The quickest thing here, and priced like it.
· Torque-converter auto — slowest shifts of the modern boxes (~200 ms, though it drives through them) and the worst driveline losses, because it drives through fluid. What it buys you is the launch: the converter stalls up against the brakes and multiplies engine torque (up to ~1.9× at a 2400 rpm stall), fading to 1:1 as the turbine catches up. That's why an automatic can out-launch a manual despite giving away power everywhere else — and why it suits a heavy, torquey car far better than a light, peaky one. Modern ones carry eight or ten gears, and the more gears a box has the wider it spans: a lower 1st to get the load moving and a taller top for cruising.

Gearing & shifting

LV1 · REAL LIFE
Each gear is a line of rpm against speed. You change up before the line hits the redline.
A bicycle's gears make the same trade a car's do: a low gear to climb from a stop, a high gear to go fast on the flat. The final drive multiplies every gear by the same amount, so it shifts the whole set up or down. Shorter gearing accelerates harder and tops out sooner; taller gearing is relaxed and, up to a point, faster.
Good drivers change up where the next gear will pull harder, which for a peaky engine is near the redline and for a diesel is well before it.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
The final drive is chosen for your engine, not by the slider. A car is geared for maximum speed when the engine sits at its power peak exactly as drag consumes that power — not at the limiter, where it is already past peak and cannot hold the speed. So the app solves the drag balance for the speed your car could theoretically hold, Ppeak·η = ½ρ·CdA·v³ + Crr·m·g·v, and picks the ratio that puts peak power there. A 3800 rpm diesel and a 9000 rpm screamer therefore get completely different ratios, as they should — before this, both got the same ones, and the diesel ran out of gear at 167 km/h while the air could not have stopped it until 219.
The GEARING slider is now a bias about that — roughly 1.28× shorter at one end for acceleration, 0.74× taller at the other for cruising, and neutral in the middle. Two consequences worth knowing. Top speed has an optimum in the middle of the slider, not at the tall end: gear taller than ideal and the engine can no longer reach the power it needs at that road speed, so the car gets slower. And on a twisty circuit short gearing costs you almost nothing, because you trade a top speed you never see for acceleration you use in every corner.
Where the car changes gear. The 0–100 and quarter-mile runs upshift where the next gear pulls harder at the wheels than the current one — the torque crossover, which is what a good driver and a good automatic both do — or at the limiter if that comes first. For a peaky engine that is right up at the redline anyway. For a diesel it is not: its torque has faded long before the limiter, and holding it there used to cost a 2.0 TDI four seconds to 100 km/h. The lap uses the same rule. One step remains and it is real: if a taller final drive pushes 100 km/h into the next gear, the run makes one more shift and the time jumps by about one shift.
The GEARING slider — moving it shortens or lengthens the final drive about the ratio the app matched to your engine: about 1.28× shorter at 0 and 0.74× taller at 100, neutral near the middle. The resulting ratio is shown next to it. Short gearing multiplies torque at the wheels, so the car accelerates harder and reaches the limiter sooner in every gear; tall gearing does the opposite. Top gear at the limiter is a hard ceiling — the limiter cuts fuel there, so no amount of power pushes the car past the speed its own gearing allows. The final drive is held to 2.2–6.0:1, the mechanical range of a real one.

Otherwise the ratios follow the textbook's progressive design (Naunheimer, Automotive Transmissions), the steps shrinking towards the top: ij = in · φ1n−j · φ2(n−j)(n−j−1)/2 with the span (1st over top) growing with the number of gears, ln(span) = 0.065 + 0.910·ln(n−1). The final drive is matched so that peak power arrives at the drag-limited speed, solving Ppeak · η = ½ ρ CdA v³ + crr m g v then biased by the GEARING slider and held to 2.2–6.0:1. The upshift rule is the wheel-torque crossover, T(N) · ig ≤ T(N · ig+1/ig) · ig+1, or the limiter, whichever comes first, the same in the 0–100 and on the lap.

Differential & traction

LV1 · REAL LIFE
An open diff sends the same torque to both wheels, so the one on ice decides how much that is.
When a car turns, the outside wheel travels further than the inside one, so the two driven wheels must be allowed to turn at different speeds. The differential does that. The catch: a simple open diff always gives both wheels the same torque, so if one wheel is on ice, or lifted in a corner, it spins and the other gets nothing either. A limited-slip diff ties the two together to stop that. A spool locks them solid: perfect for a straight line, a struggle in corners.
Weight also moves: accelerating, it shifts to the rear wheels, which is why rear-drive and four-wheel-drive cars launch harder than front-drivers.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
The differential splits drive between the two wheels on an axle — and decides how much of that axle's grip you can actually use. The two wheels are never loaded identically (road camber, engine torque reaction, axle wind-up, suspension geometry), and an open diff can only feed each wheel what the lighter-loaded one will hold: it spins that wheel and throws the rest away, leaving about 78% of the axle's grip usable at launch. That 78% is not a constant — most of the asymmetry it is fighting is torque reaction (driveshaft wind-up, anti-lift geometry, torque steer), which is proportional to the torque actually going through the axle. In first gear under full multiplication it is at its worst; in third at 100 km/h the axle sees a fraction of that torque and little more than road camber is left, so an open diff recovers toward ~94%. A viscous LSD ties the two together through fluid shear and recovers most of it (~92%); a clutch-plate LSD locks them against a preload for effectively all of it; a spool welds them solid and is the best of all in a straight line, which is why drag cars run one.
The key thing: a diff only matters while you're traction-limited. Fit a spool to a slow car and nothing changes — it never troubles its tyres. Fit one to a car that's spinning up off the line and it can be worth more than a power upgrade. Watch the LIMITED-BY behaviour: if your 0–100 barely moves when you change diff, the engine is the limit, not grip.
Why a front-driver runs out of grip so early. A FWD car only drives the front axle, which carries about 60% of the weight standing, and accelerating moves weight off it. Multiply that by an open diff (0.78) and modest tyres and a kei car is capped near 0.38 g at launch, whatever its engine makes. So a 215 hp kei reaches 100 km/h in 7.48 s and a 146 hp one in 7.45 s: both are riding the ceiling. On that 215 hp car, a clutch LSD takes it to 6.32 s, sport tyres 5.86 s, slicks 4.99 s and AWD 4.12 s, same engine throughout. The fix is grip, not power.
The trade on a lap. A locked diff never wastes drive but scrubs in every corner. On the 6.2 supercharged V8 in the muscle chassis, a spool is about 0.1 s quicker to 100 km/h than a clutch LSD (4.85 against 4.97 s) and about 1.5 s slower per lap (75.74 against 74.24 s), while an open diff is worst at both (5.96 s, 77.35 s): it spins the inside wheel just when cornering has unloaded it.
LV3 · THE PHYSICS
The two wheels of an axle are never loaded alike (camber, torque reaction, wind-up), and an open diff can only feed each the torque the lighter one holds, so the usable share of the axle's grip is 1 − 2·asym: 0.78 at launch. Most of that asymmetry is torque reaction, which scales with the torque through the axle, so an open diff recovers toward about 0.91 in a tall gear. In a corner the lateral load transfer unloads the inside wheel far more, which is what lock (0 open, 0.55 viscous, 0.80 clutch, 1 spool) fights; the price is the cornering multiplier (1.00, 0.99, 0.97, 0.90). The launch limit is Fdrive ≤ μx · Ndriven · kdiff,   ΔNrear = m · a · h / L with the drive layout fixing which axle's load counts.

Aero: drag & downforce

LV1 · REAL LIFE
Air pushes back (drag) and, with wings and a floor, pushes down (downforce).
Stick your hand out of a car window at motorway speed and you feel two things: the air pushing it back, and, if you tilt it, pushing it up or down. A car's body does the same. Drag grows with the square of speed, so the power to beat it grows with the cube: going twice as fast needs eight times the power. That is why top speed is so expensive.
Downforce from wings, splitters and a shaped floor pushes the tyres into the road. It costs some drag, but it adds grip without adding weight, and it grows with speed, so fast corners get faster.

Brakes & fade

LV1 · REAL LIFE
Braking turns speed into heat. Too much heat and the pads stop gripping: fade.
Brakes do not destroy energy, they turn it into heat. Stopping a 1500 kg car from 100 km/h puts about 580 kJ into the discs, enough to heat them by a couple of hundred degrees in a few seconds. Do it lap after lap and the discs glow; past the pads' working range their friction drops and the pedal goes long: fade. Race brakes are bigger (more metal to soak heat), vented (air pumped through vanes inside the disc), ducted, and use pads that work hot. Carbon-ceramic discs are light and shrug off 1000 °C.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
Choose the brake type on the VEHICLE tab. It sets the pads, calipers, rotor material and whether the kit has cooling ducts. The 100–0 and 0–200–0 figures and every braking zone of a lap use them, hot.
LV3 · THE PHYSICS
Force comes from the hydraulics: per wheel, two pads × pad μ × line pressure (about 100 bar in an emergency stop) × piston area × the effective radius (0.4 of the rotor diameter), over the tyre radius. Deceleration builds over about 0.2 s (Limpert). The stop is the lesser of tyre grip and brake force. The front calipers are the kit's; the rears are sized to the car, as its maker sizes them: UN ECE R13-H requires the front axle to lock first at every deceleration up to 0.8 g, so the front's share of the braking is the front axle's share of the load while braking at 0.8 g, front weight + 0.8 · h / wheelbase (about 0.55 on a 911, 0.8 on a front-heavy hatch).
Heat in is the power the brakes take out of the car, m · a · v, split by the axle loads while braking, 90% into the rotors, whose mass is the friction ring (vented on every front disc, solid at the back on the plainer kits) and the iron hat that bolts it to the hub, about a fifth more. Heat out (Limpert): convection from the faces, h = 0.04 (k/D) Re0.8 at the car's speed; through the vanes of a vented disc, a centrifugal pump moving air at 0.0158 · n · √(Do² − Di²), at a coefficient of at least the measured 0.076 · n W/m²K (27, 53 and 78 W/m²K at 342, 684 and 1025 rpm on a radial-vane rotor); and radiation. Pad friction falls from its onset temperature (street 400 °C, performance 550, track 650, carbon-ceramic 1000) by up to 35% over 150 °C. A lap starts the rotors from ambient and runs one preparation lap before the timed hot lap.
The lap is driven flat out, braking at the limit into every corner, as a professional would on a qualifying lap. Light cars and small brakes come out where real ones are measured on track (300–550 °C on a short circuit, up to ~650 °C on a fast one); heavy, fast cars on long fast circuits (a Hellcat or an E46 M3 at Spa) run their rotors to 750–900 °C and fade, as real ones do when driven that hard. Track-day measurements usually read lower, 450–600 °C in the core, because most drivers brake earlier and softer than the limit. The rear brakes stay much cooler than the fronts on most cars. The lap's driver uses every bit of both axles' grip, braking into each corner while already cornering at the limit, and there the rear tyres have nothing left for braking: ABS releases them and the fronts do it all. Real cars are measured with rears from about as hot as the fronts (a 911, a Viper) to almost cold (a Clio Cup car), because real drivers brake mostly in a straight line and turn in with some grip to spare. The lap's line is quicker than that straight-braking one in the model, by 1–2 s on a test oval.

Suspension, roll & camber

LV1 · REAL LIFE
In a corner the body leans out, and the loaded outside tyre leans with it.
Corner hard and the body rolls toward the outside. Soft suspension rolls a lot, which is comfortable over bumps but tips the outside tyre (the one doing most of the work) onto its edge. Camber is the tyre's lean seen from the front: race cars set the wheels leaning in at the top (negative camber), so that when the body rolls the loaded tyre ends up standing flat on the road.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
SUSPENSION on the VEHICLE tab: comfort, sport, coilovers or race. Stiffer setups roll less and run more static negative camber.
FRONT GEOMETRY and REAR GEOMETRY are the linkage at each end: MacPherson strut, double wishbone, multi-link, twist beam (rear only, it cannot steer) or solid axle. A real car comes with its own, and its own front and rear track; fitting another is a conversion and costs money.
FRONT SPRINGS, REAR SPRINGS, FRONT ANTI-ROLL BAR and REAR ANTI-ROLL BAR go from SOFT −2 to STIFF +2 about the kit. Stiffer all round rolls less; stiffer at one end moves the balance: a stiffer front understeers more, a stiffer rear oversteers more.

The roll centre is where the linkage takes the axle's side force. The load transfer splits in two:
  • Each axle's own mass at its own roll centre pushes on its tyres through the links. A high roll centre puts transfer on its own axle: a solid rear axle loads the rear tyres.
  • The rest is the body rolling about the line between the roll centres. That goes through the springs and bars, by the roll balance.
Each axle's transfer is taken across its own track, so a wider axle transfers less.
Balance (build 157). Each axle has its own grip:
  • its share of the weight and of the downforce
  • its share of the load transfer (set by the anti-roll bars and the roll centres), across its own track
  • its own tyres, at the camber its geometry leaves them
In a steady corner each axle has to hold the mass it carries, so the car corners only as hard as the axle that runs out first allows.
  • Front runs out first: understeer. This is a front-heavy car, because its heavily loaded front tyres grip less per kilo.
  • Rear runs out first: oversteer. This is a rear-engined 911.
Ways to move the balance:
  • Springs and bars split the elastic part of the load transfer by each axle's share of the roll stiffness: at STANDARD, the weight split. A front bar step moves about 4% of the transfer. Stiffer at the front takes grip from the front (more understeer); a softer front gives it back.
  • FRONT WIDTH fits narrower or wider fronts. A mid-engined car's narrow fronts and wide rears are what keep it from oversteering.
  • Braking moves load to the front, so on the brakes into a corner the rear runs out first. The lap brakes no harder there than the rear can hold the corner.

Performance tests

LV1 · REAL LIFE
The standard tests: lights out, 0–100, the quarter mile, then stop.
Car magazines compare cars with a few standard tests: 0–100 km/h (0–62 mph), the quarter mile (400 m) time and trap speed, top speed, and the 100–0 braking distance. They are run on a flat, dry track by a good driver, which is what the app simulates.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
The engine's mass and torque curve are added in and the Performance panel computes 0–100 km/h, 400/800/1000 m (time + trap speed), top speed, 100–0 braking and the 0–200–0 test live — a traction-limited launch and a tyre/brake-limited stop with fade, weight transfer, downforce, drag and rolling resistance. Sticky tyres, big brakes and AWD help most.

The racing line

LV1 · REAL LIFE
Out, in, out: use the whole road to make the corner as straight as possible.
The fastest way through a corner is not down the middle. A driver starts on the outside, clips the inside kerb at the apex, and lets the car run out to the edge on exit: out-in-out. That makes the arc as wide (and so as fast) as the road allows. Where a corner leads onto a long straight, drivers turn in later and apex later, giving up a little entry speed for a faster exit that pays all the way down the straight.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
The TRACK tab puts the car on a circuit and solves a lap. First it works out the racing line: nobody drives the centreline, because using the full width of the track straightens a corner and so raises the speed you can carry through it. The line is found by minimising its total curvature inside the track edges, and out-in-out through corners, straightened esses and linked complexes all fall out of that on their own. Two things bend it away from pure minimum curvature. First, the track is not one width: every circuit here narrows through its tight sections and opens out on the fast ones, and a 7 m corner simply cannot be straightened as much as a 13 m one — the figure quoted on the map is the range, and the circuit's headline width is its average, and the car's centre can come no closer to an edge than half the car's width. Second, minimum curvature is not minimum time, and the line each car drives is its own: starting from minimum curvature, the solver moves the line a little at a time, re-solves the car's speed where the move can change it, and keeps every move that makes the lap quicker. Nothing in that describes a racing line, yet the familiar shapes come out of it: a late apex where a corner opens onto a straight (exit speed is carried the whole length of what follows, while at the entry you are braking anyway), a tighter line where the next corner comes quickly, and a different line for a 90 hp hatchback than for an 800 hp aero car, because their grip, power and brakes trade differently. Circuits are three-dimensional, so the lap also accounts for elevation: gravity pulling you back up a climb and pushing you down a descent, the slightly reduced weight-on-tyres of a slope, and — the one that really bites — vertical curvature. Over a crest the car is thrown light and loses grip exactly where it is going fastest; through a compression it is pressed into the road and can carry far more speed. On the test circuits a dip is worth about a third more corner speed than the same corner on a brow. Where the hills fall matters as much as how big they are: a climb in a braking zone lets you brake later and gains time, while the same climb on a straight simply costs you.
The driver's foot. The THR bar is a pedal, not the solver's on/off. The driver moves it no faster than a foot does: fully off in 0.10 s, fully on in 0.25 s. It holds a corner steady and does not back off again once a car is driving out of a corner. On a dual-clutch, sequential or automatic gearbox it stays flat through an upshift (the gearbox cuts the spark, and the turbo stays spun up); on a manual it lifts for each shift.
Full screen. ⛶ at the start of the live-timing controls puts the lap, its live instruments and the replay controls on the whole screen, in landscape: on a phone held upright the view turns itself. ✕ (or Esc) brings it back.
Zoom and pan the map. Scroll (or pinch on a phone) to zoom in on any corner, drag to move around, and double-click to fit the whole circuit again. The track is drawn to scale, so zoomed in you can see where the racing line uses the width.
LV3 · THE PHYSICS
The line is found in two steps. First the minimum-curvature line inside the track edges (the car's centre kept half its width from each edge), which already gives out-in-out shapes. Then a minimum-time refinement for this car: the line is nudged a little at a time, the car's speed is re-solved where the nudge matters, and every nudge that makes the lap quicker is kept. The late apex, tighter lines before quick direction changes, and different lines for different cars all come out of that search without being written in.
One limit worth knowing: the lap is quasi-steady. The car is always exactly on the speed its grip, power and brakes allow at each point of the line, so there is no yaw inertia (turning in takes no time), and the driver never makes a mistake. The minimum-time line is found by small moves that each make the lap quicker, so it is the best line near minimum curvature: a very different line (taking a corner on the other side entirely) would not be found.

Solving a lap

LV1 · REAL LIFE
A lap is three limits taking turns: power on the straights, braking into corners, grip through them.
A lap is the car at one of three limits at every moment: grip in the corners, power on the straights, and braking into the corners. A driver's job is to stay right on whichever limit applies, and to blend them: start turning while still braking, start accelerating before the corner ends.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
It is worth 5–7% of lap time over driving the centreline, and a wider circuit is worth more. Then it finds the cornering speed at every point (v = √(μ·(mg + downforce)·r / m)), then works backwards from each corner to find the braking points and forwards out of each corner on the available drive, taking the lower speed at every point. A friction circle ties the two together: grip already spent turning isn't available for accelerating or braking, so a car at the cornering limit can't also put its power down. The coloured line on the map shows what is limiting the car at each point, and the legend totals how much of the lap each one accounts for — a fast circuit is mostly power and braking, a tight one mostly traction out of slow corners. Shift time is charged per gear change around the lap, so a slow-shifting box loses real time.
LV3 · THE PHYSICS
The cornering limit at each point of the line, of radius r, with downforce L(v): vmax = √( μ · (m g + L(v)) · r / m ) (with load sensitivity and load transfer applied, so it is solved, not read off). Then a backward pass from every corner finds the braking points, and a forward pass finds what the drive can do out of every corner, through the gearbox and with the turbo's real boost. The lower of the speeds wins at every point. A friction ellipse ties them: grip used for turning is not available to brake or accelerate. Resistance on the forward pass is drag, rolling resistance on the slope, gravity on the slope and cornering drag. Shift time is charged per gear change.

Elevation, crests & compressions

LV1 · REAL LIFE
Over a crest the car goes light. In a dip it is pressed into the road.
Real circuits are not flat. A climb costs speed and a descent gives it back, but the bigger effect is the shape of the road. Over a crest the car is thrown light and has less grip just when it is fastest (the Nürburgring's Flugplatz, where cars fly). Through a compression, like Spa's Eau Rouge, it is pressed into the road and can carry far more speed than the same corner on the flat.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
Every built-in circuit carries its terrain, and imported GPX tracks bring their own. Where the hills fall matters: a climb in a braking zone helps you stop and lets you brake later, the same climb on a straight just costs time.
LV3 · THE PHYSICS
On a slope θ with vertical curvature κv (positive in a dip), the load on the tyres and the force along the road are N = m (g cos θ + v² κv) + L(v)    Fslope = − m g sin θ so a crest (κv < 0) takes load off exactly where speed is highest. On the test circuits a dip is worth about a third more corner speed than the same corner on a brow. The 22 real circuits carry measured width and terrain elevation.

Importing a circuit

LV1 · REAL LIFE
Any GPS trace of a lap becomes a circuit you can drive.
Phones, watches and lap timers record where you went as a GPX file: a list of latitude, longitude and height points, one every second or so. That is enough to rebuild a circuit's shape and its hills, though not how wide it is.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
IMPORT GPX TRACK loads a real circuit from a GPX trace — a recorded lap, an export from a mapping tool, anything with track points. The trace is projected to metres, resampled to an even spacing (GPX points are wildly uneven, and a starved spline invents corners) and lightly smoothed so GPS jitter doesn't read as curvature. Elevation comes straight from the file, so an imported circuit gets its real gradients. Imported tracks are marked * in the list and are kept in your browser only — nothing is uploaded, and nothing ships with the app.
LV3 · THE PHYSICS
The points are projected from latitude and longitude to metres, resampled to even spacing (GPS points bunch and spread with speed, and a starved spline invents corners), lightly smoothed so GPS jitter does not read as curvature, closed into a loop, and fitted with a closed Catmull-Rom spline, the same representation the built-in circuits use. Corners are found as local minima of the racing line's radius.
On accuracy: an imported circuit's lap time will read a little slow. A GPX trace carries no width at all, so the width profile is modelled from the layout — roads are built wide where they are fast and narrow where they are tight — rather than surveyed. The line is the car's own minimum-time line, and the driver never makes a mistake or takes a compromise line. Treat it as a consistent yardstick for comparing your own builds, not as a real lap record.

The race

LV1 · REAL LIFE
A race is a lap, done again and again, while the tyres heat and wear, the fuel burns off and the other cars get in the way.
A race is not a string of perfect laps. Tyres have to be brought up to temperature, grip best inside a window (a road tyre is happy warm, a slick wants it hot), and wear as they slide; worn past a point they fall off a cliff. Fuel is mass: a full tank is slower than an empty one, and a car that cannot make the distance has to stop. Pit strategy trades the 20-odd seconds a stop costs against what fresh tyres win back. And there are other cars: a car close behind on a straight gets a tow (less drag in the hole the leader punches in the air), but through a corner the dirty air behind another car costs grip, which is why passing is hard.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
RACE puts two to eight cars on one circuit and runs them to a lap count. Nobody drives: you choose the cars, the circuit, the laps and the rules, press START RACE, and watch. The cars can be the one on the VEHICLE tab, any car in the garage, or any factory car, and the same car can be entered twice.
How it is worked out. Each car's lap is solved first, by the same solver the TRACK tab uses, on its own racing line: at five levels of tyre grip and three fuel loads, and once from a standing start. The race then runs every car along its line on a 50 ms clock, reading its speed off those solves for the grip and the fuel it has at that moment. A car alone on the circuit, on perfect tyres, laps in the TRACK tab's time to within a few hundredths.
Tyres. Each of the four tyres has a temperature. It rises with the work the tyre does sliding (more as it nears its grip) and rolling, and falls with the air over it, faster at speed. Each compound grips best in its own window (a road tyre ~45-110 °C, a slick ~76-120 °C). Below it the tyre has less grip, and above it the grip falls away twice as fast. Wear comes from the same sliding work, faster on a softer compound and faster again overheated; past ~70% it goes off a cliff. The tyre squares show blue when cold, green in the window, yellow and then red when hot. TYRE WARMERS start road compounds above eco at 70 °C rather than the air's temperature.
Fuel. The fuel a car burns is the power it spends at each point times its own engine's BSFC at that rpm and boost. An injected engine cuts the fuel off the throttle and a carburettor cannot. The tank is the body's (a kei car 30 L, a GT 80 L), and a car gets lighter as the fuel goes.
Pit stops. On AUTO STOPS a car plans its stops before the start, from a practice run on its own: the fewest the tank and the tyres allow, or one or two more when fresh tyres win back more than the pit lane costs. A set number of stops spreads them evenly. A car starts with the fuel for its first stint and refuels for each next one. On NO PITS it starts full and runs on one set of tyres, and a car that cannot make the distance runs dry and retires.
Traffic. A car that catches another is held up behind it until it has a few km/h more speed on a straight or into a braking zone (more on a narrow circuit), and then goes by. The tow grows the closer it runs: about 2% more speed 10 m behind on a straight, under 1% at 40 m, so a line of cars nose to tail pulls itself along and a lone leader gets caught. Within a second behind through a corner a car loses a little grip in the dirty air. Lapped cars are shown the blue flag and let the leaders through.
Racecraft. A car with a quicker car close behind may defend: it moves to the inside line, so the car behind needs a few km/h more to get by, but its own exit from the next corner is slower, and a car that defends lap after lap loses time to the cars further back. A car held up into a braking zone may try a late-braking move: it brakes later, and whether it sticks is the two cars' grip and brakes (fading brakes lose these fights) and the two drivers. If it sticks the car is through, though the car it passed has a few seconds to take the place straight back (the switchback); if not, it runs wide and loses time.
The driver. No lap is perfect: each one is drawn off the car's limit, and now and then there is a mistake in a corner, more often on tyres that are worn or too hot. Each car has a DRIVER: BALANCED (the average); AGGRESSIVE (attacks, defends and dives more, works the tyres harder, makes more mistakes); SMOOTH (closer to the limit every lap, gentle on the tyres, rarely makes a move); RAIN MASTER (up to 1.5% quicker in the wet, and steadier there); ROOKIE (inconsistent, error-prone, hard on the tyres, slower in the rain). The draws come from the SEED, so the same seed gives the same race, to the hundredth. GRID is the qualifying order (one hot lap each, on an empty tank) or that order reversed.
Weather. WEATHER is dry, damp, wet, rain later (it starts part-way through, when the seed says) or drying, and AIR sets the day's temperature; a sunny track runs ~14 °C above the air, a wet one at it. Water takes grip by compound: a treaded road tyre keeps ~70% of its dry grip, a semi-slick about half, a slick about a third. Intermediates and full wets are rain tyres for the race only: they keep more of their grip in the wet, and overheat and wear on a drying line. A car changes to the compound that leaves it most grip when the time that wins back on its own solved laps, over the laps still to go, is more than a stop costs. So with three laps left a car may sensibly stay out on the wrong tyres. The tyres also cool in the spray, and run cooler on a wet surface.
Brakes. The rotors run the lap solver's own heat model all race long: they heat with every stop, cool on the straights and in the pit box, and carry their temperature from lap to lap. Past their pads' onset they fade, and a fading car brakes earlier: its braking zones stretch by how much less it can stop with. The cards show front and rear rotors and the fade.
Each car's plan. Under each car: STOPS (automatic, or none, one, two or three, or no pits at all), how hard it is driven (PUSH: closer to the limit every lap, more mistakes; SAVE FUEL: lifting before the braking zones, ~10% less fuel, a little slower, fewer mistakes), the TYRES it starts on, and the WET CALL (change for the weather, or stay out).
The page. RACE opens as a page of its own: the map, and beside it (below it on a phone) the setup, then the race. ◀ SIM (or Esc) goes back to where you were, and ⛶ makes it properly full screen.
Watching. The map shows every car, and the rain when it falls. The timing tower gives the gap to the leader, the last lap and the best (purple for the fastest of the race), each car's tyres and its stops. Click a car in it to follow that car. The cards under it show each car's tyres, fuel and brakes. The speed runs from 1× to 100×, and the bar under the controls scrubs through the race.
The report. REPORT PNG / PDF saves the race on one sheet: the classification with each car's stints, a lap chart, every car's lap times with the wet shaded in behind, the stints by compound, and the key moments: passes, stops, mistakes, the rain.
What is a rule and not physics: overtaking, the tow, dirty air, the pit lane's time loss, driver error, and when the rain comes. The cars also share one racing line and do not fight for space on it; a pass is drawn alongside. The wet-grip, tyre-window and wear figures are fitted.
LV3 · THE PHYSICS
Pace comes from the lap solver. Each car's lap is solved up front on its own racing line at five grip levels (0.72 to 1.00 of its tyres at their best; eight, down to 0.42, when the race can be wet) and three fuel loads (empty, half, full), plus once from a standing start. The race then moves every car along its line on a 50 ms clock, reading its speed off that grid for the grip and fuel it has at that moment.
Tyres are one lump each, loaded as the car's own weight transfer and downforce say: C · dT/dt = 0.03 · F · v · u + 0.8 · crr · N · v − A · (12 + 7 v0.8 + 150 w) · (T − Tair) − 10 A · (T − Ttrack) slip work (rising with u, the share of the tyre's grip in use) and rolling hysteresis in; convection to the air, the water on a wet track (w, its wetness) and conduction into the surface out, with the track ~14 °C above the air when dry. Wet grip is 1 − (w / wwet) · (1 − keep), keep being the compound's share of its grip on a wet track (0.70 a road tyre, 0.36 a slick, 0.82 a full wet); a car changes compound when the lap time that wins back, read off its own solved laps, times the laps left beats the stop. Grip from temperature is 1 − k · ((T − Topt)/Twin)², with k = 0.03 cold and 0.12 hot, so the same loss comes in half the distance above the window as below it (grip falls away twice as fast hot), floored at 0.72. Wear follows the slip work, faster on a softer compound, and above the window it is multiplied by 1 + 3 × how far over (in windows) the tyre runs; and costs grip as 1 − 0.06 w − 1.2 · max(0, w − 0.7)²: the cliff.
Fuel burned is the power spent at each point times that engine's BSFC at its rpm and boost, out of a tank sized by body, and the car is lighter as it goes.
Brakes run the lap solver's rotor model (heat in from the braking power, convection and radiation out, the vanes pumping air) every step of the race, so their temperature carries from lap to lap; where the rotors fade, a braking zone runs to its apex as v² = vapex² + (fade now / fade on the solved lap) · (v² − vapex²).
Rules, not physics: the pit lane costs 20 s plus refuelling at 2.5 L/s and 6 s for tyres; the tow is 0.45 · e−gap/25 m of the drag, worth ~2% at 10 m, dirty air −1.5% within a second through a corner; defending costs 1% through the next corner; a late-braking move sticks with 0.4 + 2 · (grip difference) + 1.5 · (fade difference) + 0.3 · (aggression difference) − 0.15 against a defending car; a pass needs about 4 km/h more on a 12 m wide circuit (more on a narrower one); each lap is drawn up to 0.6% off the limit with a 1.2% chance of a mistake, more on tired tyres, faded brakes, in the wet and when pushing. When the rain comes and how fast a line dries are drawn from the seed. Cars share one racing line. The tyre temperature, window, wear and wet-grip constants are fitted to published ranges. Fuel is a flat-out driver's: over a lap at the limit the engines run at a lap-average BSFC of 265–325 g/kWh (25–31% efficient), on full throttle half the lap, and burn 19 L/100 km (an MX-5) to 71 (a Hellcat) at Spa. Owners report about the same for mid-size cars (an E46 M3 or a Civic Type R ~35 L/100 km on track) and 20–40% less for big-power cars, because they drive below the limit.

Engine shops hand their customers a dyno sheet: the power and torque curves, peaks, and the conditions the run was made in. The app produces both.

The scorecard

LV1 · REAL LIFE
Seven marks, one grade. Balance beats any single number.
Engineers compare engines by more than peak power. Specific output (power and torque per litre) shows how hard the engine works its size; a wide powerband and strong low-down torque make it easy to drive; efficiency is fuel bills and range; a knock margin is reliability; emissions decide whether it can be sold at all. A great engine is good at all of them.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
Every build is graded S → F from seven things: specific power & torque (per litre), powerband width, thermal efficiency, knock safety, driveability (low-end torque) and emit (emissions). Chasing peak power usually costs drivability, safety and emissions — the score rewards a balanced build.

Objectives & budget

LV1 · REAL LIFE
Every part has a price. A good engineer meets the brief, not just the stopwatch.
Real engineers almost never get to build the fastest thing they can. They get a brief: a power target, a fuel-economy limit, an emissions standard, a price. Meeting all of it at once is the job, and the cheapest part that does the job is usually the right one.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
Track objectives judge the whole car, not just the engine: four of them set a lap target on a named circuit (Club Sprint at Ashdown, Grand Prix Pace at Riverside, Under Seven at the Ring on the Nordschleife, and Lap per Dollar for the cheapest car that can still lap quickly). Their budget covers engine plus vehicle, so a cheap engine in an expensive chassis counts against you — build the car on the VEHICLE tab, then watch the objective card.

Options, sound & tips

LV1 · REAL LIFE
An engine's sound is its firing pulses, shaped by the pipes they run down.
An engine's sound is mostly its exhaust pulses: every time a cylinder's exhaust valve opens, a pressure pulse bangs down the pipe. How the pulses are spaced (the firing order), which pipe they go down (one per bank on a V8), how long the pipes are and what silencer they end in is why a flat-plane Ferrari V8 screams, an American V8 burbles and an inline six sounds smooth. Around that you hear the rest of the car: turbo whistle, supercharger whine and intake roar.
LV2 · IN THE GAME
ENGINE SOUND — two models, picked under ENGINE SOUND MODEL. SYNTH, the default, is described here. GAS simulates the gas instead, the way Ange Yaghi's engine-sim does: every cylinder, its intake and exhaust runners and each bank's collector are volumes of gas with pressure, temperature and flow between them, and the sound is the pressure in the exhaust runners, run through a recording of a real exhaust. It is heavier on the processor. A two-stroke gets its own model, with piston ports and a crankcase that pumps the charge; a rotary gets three chambers to a rotor breathing through ports the rotor uncovers. Either is heard on the DYNO tab, following the engine controls, and on the TRACK tab, following the car round the lap replay (silent while the replay is paused). It is built from the firing pulses: every cylinder fires at its place in the firing order and its pulse goes down its own bank's exhaust, left and right for a V or a boxer. That is where a cross-plane V8's burble comes from, and why an inline six sounds smooth. Then the pulses run the exhaust you fitted: the header rings at its own length (a race header cut for high revs is shorter and sings higher; a cast manifold barely rings), the system behind it drones, and the silencer, the cat and a turbo, if there is one, quieten it. Load is in the pulses themselves: each one is as hard as the charge it burned, so flat out is louder and raspier than a cruise, and a fuel cut leaves only a hollow whoosh. A wild cam makes the idle lope, an idle thing that fades as the throttle opens. Lift off a carburetted engine after a pull and it crackles while the pipe is hot (carburettors cannot cut the fuel, so the thin overrun charge misfires and lights in the exhaust); an injected one stays quiet, and anti-lag pops on purpose. Around the exhaust you also hear the rest of the car: a turbo whines at its compressor's blade-pass pitch as the shaft spools, and hisses through its blow-off valve when you lift (longer from a big intercooler); a supercharger whirrs with the crank, mostly once its boost comes in (off boost a Roots or a screw blower's bypass is open), and a centrifugal whistles with its impeller speed; a turbo's whine and blow-off are as big as its compressor, so a small one only hisses; anti-lag bangs and crackles on every lift; the intake roars at its runner's tuned pitch, loudest through open stacks; and a chamber that knocks rings at about 6 kHz, which is a diesel's rattle on every stroke (worst cold) and a petrol engine's warning when it is past its knock limit.
WEAR & DAMAGE — with wear on, detonation, overheating, running lean under boost and over-revving damage the engine and can blow it; with it off the engine is invincible, which is the way to experiment.
LANGUAGE — English or Greek.
Tip: turn WEAR off in OPTIONS to experiment freely, then back on for consequences.
LV3 · THE PHYSICS
Sound is synthesised from the same model, in an AudioWorklet running its own crank clock:
· each cylinder fires at its slot in the real firing order, into its bank's pipe (left and right for a V or boxer);
· the header primary rings at its quarter-wave, f = c / (4 L), as sharply as the header's length spread allows, and the system behind it adds a half-wave drone; silencer, cat and turbine take off the top;
· each pulse is as strong as the charge burned (manifold pressure × fuel), and steepens (rasps) when strong;
· a turbo whines at its blade-pass frequency from the live shaft speed, a blower from the crank through its drive;
· the intake roars at the runner's Helmholtz frequency;
· knock rings the chamber at f = 1.84 c / (π B), about 6 kHz, through the block.

Known limits

What the model leaves out, said out loud.
This is a physics model, and like every model it leaves things out. These are the ones worth knowing, because they can move a result you care about. Each one is also noted on the page it belongs to.
· Two-strokes and rotaries are partly fitted. Their work rests on measured type factors, their breathing on a fitted VE curve, and their knock on an older points score. Four-stroke poppet-valve engines, petrol and diesel, are derived (Engine types).
· Twin-scroll manifolds are not rewarded, though each cylinder's blowdown pulse now feeds the turbine (Turbochargers).
· A turbo cannot fail from overspeed. Its tip speed is a cap the model respects; nothing breaks if you exceed what a real cartridge survives.
· Some hardware is hand-set to real relative behaviour rather than derived: the induction systems, the supercharger boost laws, the nitrous shot, the MBT spark rule, the friction curve's shape, the cooling-system model (Induction & throttle, Superchargers, Nitrous oxide, Ignition & spark timing, Friction, oil & pumping, Heat, cooling & EGT).
· Designed parts leave two things out: a silencer's own back-pressure, and the intake plenum's volume (Designing your own parts).
· Emissions are trend correlations, not chemistry (Emissions & the catalyst). Wear rates are game rules (Wear & reliability). The scorecard's weights are design choices (The scorecard).
· No vibration or balance is modelled (Crank & firing order).
· The lap is quasi-steady: the car is always exactly at the speed grip, power and brakes allow, turning in takes no time (no yaw inertia). Each axle's grip is its own, so a car understeers or oversteers (build 157), but only as a limit: it never slides or spins, the driver never makes a mistake, and the line is the best one near minimum curvature (The racing line).
· A lap is a single hot lap: no tyre wear, no fuel burn, no traffic. A The race adds all three, but its traffic, overtaking, tow, dirty air, pit losses and driver errors are rules, and its tyre temperature and wear constants are fitted.
· Imported circuits have modelled width, because a GPX file carries none (Importing a circuit).
· The reference cars are matched, not reproduced: the 0–100 and top speed errors on How we check against reality are the honest size of what is left.

How we check against reality

A model derived from first principles cannot be tuned to fit, so it is checked against real cars afterwards.
A derived model cannot be tuned until it matches, so the only honest way to judge it is to compare it with real cars afterwards and accept the answer. The project's test suite (tests/, run headless against this very page) does that:
· Acceleration (test41): 0–100 km/h for six real cars, from a 64 hp Suzuki Alto Works to a 460 hp BMW M2, with power, mass, driveline and gearbox matched. Currently 9.68% RMS; the model is mostly slower than the real cars (the Alto within 0.1%, the A80 Supra 16% slow).
· Top speed (aero): seven real cars with drag area matched, cross-checked against an independent closed-form solve of P = (½ρCdA v² + crr m g) v. Currently 6.5% RMS.
· Self-consistency (test43): the acceleration run and the lap must agree about the same car.
· Engines (test46–48): presets modelled on real engines are checked for power, torque, where they peak, and exhaust temperature against published figures, with the remaining gaps named and budgeted.
Most other tests check behaviour that is true of real cars: a bigger turbo spools later, an LSD beats an open diff off the line, a climb costs time, a richer mixture runs cooler. Those catch a lot, but they are blind to a model that is wrong in the same direction everywhere, which is why the real-car comparisons exist.
